Four dollar objects

Payment stablecoins, tokenized deposits, deposit tokens, and yield wrappers look similar in a wallet but diverge in reserve structure, legal treatment, and crisis behavior. The frameworks below exist because each object needs different tests, different monitoring, and different escalation paths.

Framework stack

The frameworks work in sequence.

1. Define the object

Is this a payment stablecoin, a tokenized deposit, or a yield wrapper? The answer determines the starting rulebook.

2. Test the product at launch

MVEP asks whether legal, operational, and economic equivalence are actually proven now. Ten diligence categories, each with a named artifact and a pass/fail test.

3. Monitor the control layer after approval

CLII tracks whether the gateway layer's compliance posture is degrading over time. Approval starts the monitoring cycle; CLII is the drift detector.

4. Map balance-sheet and system-level consequences

The Credit Migration Model asks where deposits move, how reserve growth changes funding quality, and which bank balance sheets absorb the shift. The essay's term "deposit mutation" is the author's synthesis of the Federal Reserve's December 2025 argument that stablecoins can reduce, recycle, or restructure deposits, including by shifting bank funding toward more concentrated, uninsured, wholesale balances.

5. Identify who controls the operating layer right now

The Regime Dashboard tracks routing defaults, sweep ownership, hold authority, disclosure burden, and where compliance has actually attached. CLARITY-style provisions (House-passed form analyzed here) are the current policy input.

Core frameworks

Monitoring · Post-approval drift detection

CLII (Control Layer Intensity Index)

What it does. Measures how heavily regulated each gateway is across five dimensions: licensing, reserve transparency, freeze capability, compliance infrastructure, and geographic restrictions. Each gateway receives a weighted score from 0 to 1.

What it is for. Ongoing monitoring after a product is approved. Tracks whether a gateway's custody arrangements, transaction screening, reconciliation practices, or volume concentration are changing in ways that should trigger review.

Stress performance is a separate question. A high CLII score means a gateway faces more regulatory obligations. Stress performance depends on reserve-bank exposure, counterparty concentration, and other entity-specific conditions that sit outside what the CLII measures. Circle (CLII 0.92) and Coinbase (CLII 0.85) had near-identical scores but showed sharply different stress retention during SVB.

What should trigger re-evaluation. A gateway crossing from one tier to another (for example Tier 1 to Tier 2), a significant drop in any single dimension score, a jump in volume concentration through one gateway, or a new dependency where traffic that previously spread across multiple gateways now routes through one.

Re-evaluation outcomes. Reaffirmation (no change needed), conditional continuation (with a defined remediation timeline), or suspension of new issuance until the issue is resolved.

Diligence · Pre-launch entry gate

MVEP (Minimum Viable Equivalence Pack)

What it does. Tests whether a tokenized product preserves three equivalence claims: legal (does the holder have the same rights?), operational (does the infrastructure perform the same functions?), and economic (does the product behave the same way under stress?).

What it is for. Entry-gate diligence before launch or committee approval. A product that cannot demonstrate equivalence across all three claims is not ready for institutional adoption.

How it works. A ten-category diligence pack for testing whether a tokenized product preserves the legal rights, operational resilience, and economic behavior of the traditional asset under stress. Covers rights parity, system of record, reconciliation, custody, insolvency posture, settlement finality, exception handling, governance, disclosures, and collateral operability. For tokenized Treasuries, the paper identifies three liquidation paths, four constraints on path availability, and seven stress scenarios.

What should trigger re-evaluation. A missing artifact, a failed test, a failure sequence with no documented recovery plan, or a situation where it is unclear who absorbs losses if the product fails.

Relationship to CLII. MVEP is the entry gate (is equivalence proven now?). CLII is the drift detector (is the control layer degrading over time?). A product can pass MVEP at launch and subsequently fail one of its categories; CLII flags that drift.

Deposit analysis · Funding-structure shift

Credit Migration Model In development

What it does. Tracks where deposit balances sit and how they move as stablecoin reserve growth scales.

What it is for. Understanding whether reserve growth reduces, recycles, or restructures deposits; which banks gain or lose funding; and how funding quality changes even when aggregate deposits hold steady.

Why it matters. The policy question is whether bank funding becomes more concentrated (fewer banks hold larger shares), more skewed toward institutional depositors than retail, and more vulnerable to rapid withdrawal as stablecoin-serving banks absorb reserve balances. That is the mechanism the Federal Reserve highlights in its December 2025 note. The essay's term "deposit mutation" is the author's synthesis of that argument; the Fed describes the underlying mechanism as deposits being reduced, recycled, or restructured.

What should trigger re-evaluation. A change in which assets issuers hold as reserves, concentration of reserves at fewer banks, a shift in how redemptions are processed, or a change in which banks absorb new reserve inflows.

Control layer · Operating-regime mapping

Regime Dashboard

What it does. Classifies who controls the operating layer right now: who owns the interface, who sets the default routing, who holds the authority to freeze or delay transactions, and who bears the compliance obligations.

What it is for. Tracking where value capture, liability, and compliance actually sit in a live product: routing defaults, sweep ownership, hold authority, fee structures, and disclosure obligations.

Why it matters. CLARITY-style provisions (House-passed form analyzed here) attach compliance where it becomes operational: at the interface, in the routing defaults, in the hold and release workflows, and in the audit logs. The question is which entity becomes the one that regulators hold accountable for compliance, and whether that entity also captures the economics (fees, float, data) or whether compliance obligations and economic benefits end up at different points in the stack.

What should trigger re-evaluation. A shift in who owns the front-end interface, who controls default routing, where yield is allowed to live, or where hold and reporting obligations attach.

Market structure · Equilibrium classification

Tokenized Dollar Regime Classifier

What it does. Classifies the current U.S. dollar operating-layer equilibrium along two axes: control-layer dominance (how much of the economic surface is captured by interfaces, routers, and sweep products that regulators attach obligations to) and issuer dominance (how much accrues to the stablecoin or deposit-token issuer layer itself). Produces one of three regime labels: Control-dominant, Issuer-dominant, or Split / Contested.

What it is for. Market-structure classification over time. Which layer of the tokenized-dollar stack is capturing the economics, who bears the regulatory burden, and whether policy changes are shifting the equilibrium. Not a safety or solvency model; tracks structural power allocation.

How it works. Each axis scored 0 to 6 via three measurable components. ControlScore captures obligated-interface share of flows, default sweep and wrapper penetration, and holds or freeze usage as operating norm. IssuerScore captures settlement-object concentration, reserve carry retention, and direct mint and burn authority. Classification rule uses a 3-month rolling average (updated weekly in stress): Control-dominant if ControlScore is 4 or more and exceeds IssuerScore by 2 or more; Issuer-dominant by the mirror test; Split / Contested otherwise.

What should trigger re-evaluation. Three or more of five high-beta policy signposts moving in the same direction within a quarter (GENIUS implementation rulemaking; CBDC posture; bank deposit-token launches; offshore perimeter tightening; committee leadership plus markup calendar changes); or two or more component scores flipping within a quarter.

Relationship to CLII and MVEP. Complementary, not substitute. CLII scores per-gateway regulatory posture (entity-level). MVEP scores per-product institutional diligence. Regime Classifier scores the market-structure equilibrium (system-level) those entities operate in. All three can be computed for the same universe of operators; each answers a different question.

How the work runs

Four operating principles behind every framework on this page.

Principle · Source discipline

Evidence-led

Every claim ties to a source, a threshold, or a tested condition. No claim ships without one.

Principle · Stress-first orientation

Stress-first design

Each framework names where stress enters first, which thresholds force re-evaluation, and what evidence should change the view.

Principle · Accountability

Named decision owners

Every framework points to who approves, who monitors, who escalates, and what event forces action.

Principle · Reproducibility

Versioned and replicable

Frameworks publish when the evidence is strong enough to support real operating decisions. Manuscripts and code travel with them when possible.

Built so allocators, regulators, and operators can trace every framework decision back to its trigger, its owner, and its evidence.
