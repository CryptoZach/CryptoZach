Federal Reserve R-1835 comment: adopt the coordination provisions the sibling rules carry, and give content to the term the protection turns on

Docket No. R-1835, RIN 7100-AG78 (91 Fed. Reg. 42363, July 9, 2026; FR Doc 2026-13919) · 12 CFR Part 208, revising and republishing Section 208.63 · Comment period: July 9, 2026 to September 8, 2026

Status: filed by email on September 21, 2026. The Board email channel does not issue a regulations.gov tracking number, and none is recorded. The PDF linked here is the as-filed artifact.

Four agencies are writing one anti-money-laundering program rule four times, and the only substantive divergence between the Board's version and its three siblings is the coordination mechanism itself. The OCC, FDIC and NCUA proposals each carry two provisions requiring consultation with and information sharing with FinCEN, at proposed 12 CFR 21.21(h) and (i), 326.8(h) and (i), and 748.2(h) and (i) respectively. The Board's proposed Section 208.63 contains no such provision. The Board's own Section IV.G.2 identifies the siblings' provisions and invites comment on whether to include the same or similar ones, which is Question 23. Its Alternatives Considered section then states that the proposed rule does include provisions requiring FinCEN's consultation on supervisory actions, which is inconsistent with both Section IV.G.2 and the regulatory text as proposed. Nine recommendations address that omission, the term the proposal's principal benefit to supervised institutions depends on and never defines, and a scope mismatch between the operative text and the regulatory flexibility analysis that runs against the institutions rather than for them.

What the rule proposes

The Board proposes to revise and republish 12 CFR 208.63, the anti-money-laundering and countering-the-financing-of-terrorism program requirement for state member banks. It is the Board's contribution to a four-part rulemaking: FinCEN issued the program rule for financial institutions generally, and the OCC, the FDIC and the NCUA each issued a parallel rule for the institutions they supervise. The Board's proposal restructures the program requirement around a risk assessment, adds an effectiveness standard for independent testing, and adds a new paragraph (g) stating an enforcement-policy position: absent a significant or systemic failure, a deficiency identified in a bank's program will not itself be the basis for a significant AML or CFT supervisory action. That paragraph is the proposal's principal benefit to supervised institutions, and everything in this comment that concerns paragraph (g) concerns whether it can be relied on.

Two adjacency traps are worth naming, because the identifiers in this space are close enough to substitute for one another silently. This docket, R-1835, is not Docket R-1885, which is the Board's identifier on the separate joint FinCEN rulemaking on permitted payment stablecoin issuer customer identification. And the April 10, 2026 three-agency re-issuance at 91 Fed. Reg. 18304 carries RINs 1557-AF14, 3064-AF34 and 3133-AG08, not R-1835 or 7100-AG78.

The problem identified

Three problems, and they share a shape: in each, the codified text and the explanatory material around it say different things, and the codified text is what an examiner reads.

First, coordination. The provisions that would require the Board to consult with FinCEN before a supervisory action, and to share information with it, exist in all three sibling proposals and not in this one. The Board invites comment on whether to add them. Its own Alternatives Considered section, in the passage explaining why it chose this proposal over reissuing the 2024 notice, describes the proposal as already including them. A rulemaking whose stated purpose includes supervisory consistency across four agencies should not diverge from its siblings on precisely the provision that produces consistency, and should not describe itself two ways.

Second, the undefined trigger. Proposed paragraph (g)(1) conditions the enforcement-policy protection on the absence of a significant or systemic failure. That phrase appears eight times in the notice and is defined nowhere in it. Section 208.63(a) defines four other terms and not this one. The condition is doubly qualified, because (g)(1) routes through paragraph (d)'s requirement that the program comply in all material respects. An institution cannot plan around a protection whose trigger has no stated content, and an examiner cannot apply it consistently across districts.

Third, scope integrity. Every operative paragraph of proposed Section 208.63 runs to a state member bank. The regulatory flexibility analysis, however, describes the proposal as applying to three populations, state member banks plus Edge and agreement corporations plus the branches, agencies and representative offices of foreign banks other than a federal branch or agency or an FDIC-insured state branch, and it costs the proposal over 464 small entities on that basis. The amendatory instruction revises only Section 208.63. The current CFR provisions that carry Section 208.63 to the other two populations, 211.5(m)(1) and 211.24(j)(1), each incorporate it for program development and administration, and proposed paragraph (g) is not a program-development requirement. The asymmetry runs against the institutions: the obligations carry to all three populations, and the protection may not.

Recommendations

  1. Adopt the two FinCEN consultation and information-sharing provisions (Question 23; new paragraphs in Section 208.63). The signature contribution. All three sibling rules contain them, at proposed 12 CFR 21.21(h) and (i) for the OCC, 326.8(h) and (i) for the FDIC, and 748.2(h) and (i) for the NCUA. This is the only substantive divergence between this rule and its siblings, and it is the coordination mechanism itself.
  2. State the weighing factors for a significant or systemic failure, and retain the breadth of what the protection covers (Questions 6, 7, 21, 22, 24 and 26, and Question 5; Sections 208.63(a) and (g)). The second signature contribution, and deliberately two-directional. On the trigger: state the factors that bear on the judgment rather than a bright line, which would create the cliff effects the Board itself rejects in its Alternatives Considered section, drawing on the notice's own Section IV.C.2 examples, and answer Question 5's program-level versus component-level question because it governs the same trigger. The control-layer factor this program adds: a failure at a shared third-party dependency can be systemic in effect while presenting at any single institution as isolated. On the other half, the recommendation is contrarian and asks the Board to change nothing. The definition of a significant AML or CFT supervisory action describes what the institution is protected from, so narrowing it shrinks the shield; striking unsafe or unsound practices or conditions from it would let an action be recharacterized as a safety-and-soundness matter and escape paragraph (g) entirely. Breadth there is already bounded by the definition's conjunctive third element and its express examiner-observation carve-out. The American Bankers Association asked the sibling agencies for the same clarification of the trigger a month before this proposal published, and observed that uncertainty about eligibility means the protection will not benefit banks.
  3. Answer the harmonious-function question by enumeration, not intent (Question 8). Enumerate each divergence between the Board's final rule and the FinCEN and Agencies final rules, and state the reconciliation for each, rather than stating an intention to align. The second half of Question 8 is answered from the notice's own footnote 40 statutory derivation, which contrasts 31 U.S.C. 5318(h)(1), where a program shall be established, with 12 U.S.C. 1818(s)(1), where it is established and maintained.
  4. Key the effective date to the last of the four final rules (Questions 28 and 29). Key it to the last of the four to issue, or state the interim compliance expectation, because an institution cannot sequence one program against four rules with independent effective dates. On Question 28 the letter declines formal size tailoring, agreeing with the Board's cliff-effect reasoning, while noting that a commensurate-with standard should scale to the risk presented rather than to asset size.
  5. Reconcile the operative scope with the regulatory flexibility analysis, and state one small-entity figure (regulatory flexibility analysis; Section 208.63). Two drafting paths are offered: a defined term in paragraph (a), or amending 211.5(m) and 211.24(j) in the same final rule. Separately, the analysis states the small-entity population two ways, approximately 439 at 91 Fed. Reg. 42379 and 464 in both cost computations at 42380. The arithmetic reconciles only with 464: 32 hours times 51.20 dollars times 464 entities is 760,217.6, printed as 760,218, and 8 times 51.20 times 464 is 190,054.4, printed as 190,054; at 439 entities the same arithmetic yields 719,257.6 and 179,814.4. The letter takes no position on which figure is correct and does not assert the estimate is materially wrong. It asks the final rule to state one number and use it in both places, which is responsive to the notice's own request that commenters describe the number of small entities affected.
  6. Confirm that the rule's own distribution-channels definition reaches programmatic and control-layer arrangements (Questions 10, 11, 15 and 20; Sections 208.63(c)(1)(i)(A) and (C), and (e)). Confirm, rather than add a category: the Board already defines distribution channels as the methods and tools through which a bank opens accounts and provides products or services, including remote and other non-face-to-face means, and already names blockchain monitoring and analytics among encouraged innovative approaches. Confirm that the definition reaches tokenized and programmatic channels and third-party control-layer arrangements, and that a change in those arrangements can require a prompt risk-assessment update. On the Question 15 alternatives, retain promptly over a set schedule and significantly over materially, the latter because it would import a securities and accounting term of art. On Question 20(b), the same control-layer change that triggers a prompt update is the natural re-approval trigger, so the two questions should share one test rather than each carrying its own. The letter answers the objection that distribution channels is undefined and will evolve directly: the term is undefined in the codified text and defined only in the preamble, which is exactly why confirming it is the ask, and the evolution concern cuts toward under-inclusion rather than rigidity.
  7. Anchor independent testing and the effectiveness determination in observable criteria (Questions 1, 2 and 17; Section 208.63(c)(2)). Carry the notice's own objective-criteria language into the determination, along with its own statement that a program meeting the technical requirements on their face is not what the rule intends. On Question 2, frame the resource-allocation measure as coverage of identified risk rather than as input volume.
  8. Treat banking a permitted payment stablecoin issuer as a distinct channel (Questions 9, 11 and 14). Confirm that a bank holding reserves for, or banking, a permitted payment stablecoin issuer evaluates that relationship as a distinct distribution channel, because the illicit-finance activity occurs among holders who are not the bank's customers and whose transactions never pass through the bank's systems.
  9. Make the required accessibility run to authority and information, not location (Question 18; Section 208.63(c)(3)). Clarify that the accessibility the rule requires of the designated officer runs to that officer's authority and access to information, not to physical location alone, where AML and CFT functions or control-layer operations sit offshore. This has particular force for the covered United States offices of foreign banks, which interacts with the scope recommendation above.

A note rather than a recommendation. Section IV of the comment records two features of the notice of record without asking the Board to do anything about either. The numbered Request for Comment list runs 1 through 24, then 26, then 28 and 29: Questions 25 and 27 are absent from the published notice, in a section that expressly encourages commenters to reference specific question numbers. And Question 22 is word for word identical to Question 7, while Question 21 differs from Question 6 only by a dropped qualifier, each pair appearing once under Section IV.C and once under Section IV.G. So 29 numbers yield 27 published questions and 25 distinct ones. Both were verified by extracting the text independently from the Government Publishing Office PDF and the GPO HTML of 91 Fed. Reg. 42363 to 42382, which agree, so the gap is in the notice of record rather than in a derived copy. The duplication is substantively informative: the question asked twice is the one about the terms paragraph (g) depends on, which is the second recommendation.

A note on scope and evidence

Every operative provision and every numbered question this comment quotes is quoted from the notice text acquired in full from the Government Publishing Office, and 12 CFR 211.5(m) and 211.24(j) are quoted from the current Code of Federal Regulations. The docket history is measured rather than recalled: Docket R-1835 and RIN 7100-AG78 carry exactly two documents, the four-agency joint proposal at 89 Fed. Reg. 65242 of August 9, 2024, which closed October 8, 2024, and this Board-only notice at 91 Fed. Reg. 42363. Where the comment draws on the program's control-layer work, it does so to name a factor the Board's own framework already admits, not to make a claim about any named institution.

Program role

This comment addresses the Board of Governors on its own rulemaking. It carries no overall letter count and no agency count in its body, deliberately: both would be order-dependent on the sequence in which the program's filings land, and an earlier draft's characterization of this as the program's first comment to the Board was removed on that ground before rendering. The Board was already a party to the joint FinCEN rulemaking on which the program filed at Docket FINCEN-2026-0101 on August 21, 2026, so the Board's engagement does not depend on this comment.

The relationship to the program's April joint anti-money-laundering comment is worth stating precisely, because it is easy to overstate. That letter's Recommendation 6 asked the three participating agencies, the OCC, the FDIC and the NCUA, for three provisions in their final rule, and it stated expressly that the program took no position on whether the Board should join the rulemaking or issue its own parallel rule. The Board is therefore not an agency that was asked to act, and its separate rulemaking is the one path that recommendation declined to advocate. No causation is claimed here. What is true is narrower and still useful: the program identified the supervisory-consistency question in April and took no position on how it should be resolved, and the Board proceeding separately does not resolve that question so much as present it in the concrete form the Board itself put as Question 23.
