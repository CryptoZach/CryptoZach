SEC Regulation Crypto Assets comment: an observable control condition for the Rule 400 safe harbor, not the issuer's representations alone

Docket summary

Proposed Regulation Crypto Assets would add a new 17 CFR part 228, Rules 100 through 500, with new Forms 1-CRYPTO, 1-KC, 1-SC, 1-UC, TR and NOR: a tailored offering regime for crypto assets with a startup exemption and a fundraising exemption, principles-based disclosure under Rule 103, and a safe harbor in Rule 400 for determining when a covered investment contract ceases to exist. Under proposed Rule 400(a) the issuer must have "completed or otherwise permanently ceased all essential managerial efforts that it represented or promised it would engage in" and must not be making, or intend to make, new representations or promises; Rule 400(b) requires a Form TR carrying a certification and a supporting analysis. On satisfaction, the crypto asset is deemed not to constitute, represent, or be subject to an investment contract for purposes of Section 2(a)(1) of the Securities Act and Section 3(a)(10) of the Exchange Act. Rule 400 would codify in part the Commission's 2026 Interpretation (91 Fed. Reg. 13714, March 23, 2026), issued together with guidance from the CFTC. The proposing release (Release Nos. 33-11434 and 34-106150) was published August 21, 2026 (91 Fed. Reg. 54510), carries 154 numbered questions, and its comment period closes October 20, 2026.

Macro context

The program's second comment to the SEC, after its July 2, 2026 comment on the Draft Strategic Plan (File Number DSP-3), which recommended that the Commission measure decentralization from current on-chain state wherever a boundary or jurisdictional determination turns on it; proposed Rule 400 is the first rule text in which that recommendation has concrete purchase, and this comment supplies the operational detail the strategic-plan comment could only name. Recommendation 8 carries forward the effective-control position of the program's FinCEN AML/CFT comment and its FDIC AG29 comment. The letter carries a recommendation routing table keyed to the release's numbered questions, suggested rule text for six of the eight recommendations, a cross-agency alignment and divergence table, anticipated objections, and a sources appendix. The SEC was already engaged, so the filing adds a docket and no agency.

Proposed Rule 400 conditions a permanent change in a crypto asset's status under the Federal securities laws on facts that are measurable from public data, but tests them only through the issuer's own certification about the issuer's own prior promises. Question 131 asks whether there is a more objective standard; the comment answers that there is, and that its core is a definition of control the Commission has already written. Control is measurable, but in the present tense: in the program's fifty-two-protocol live-governance audit, launch insider allocation does not predict where governance concentration settles (Pearson r = 0.09), while current insider retention carries information (Spearman rho = 0.44). Eight recommendations follow, six of them with suggested rule text.

Recommendations

  1. Add an observable control condition to Rule 400(a) (proposed Rule 400(a); Questions 124, 127, 131 and 133). Both operative facts in Rule 400(a) as proposed are internal to the issuer: whether it has ceased the essential managerial efforts it represented or promised, and whether it intends to make new promises. Add a third condition, satisfied where neither the issuer nor any person acting for it holds a power to alter the associated crypto network or the subject crypto asset that can be exercised without holder approval and takes effect without a timelock during which holders may reject it. That is a yes-or-no fact the issuer already knows and a reviewer can check from the chain. The condition imports no market conception of decentralization: it tests one element of the Commission's own definition of a decentralized crypto system, operational control, in its narrowest, unilateral form.
  2. Require the Form TR analysis and specify its minimum content (proposed Rule 400(b) and Form TR; Questions 128 and 130). Do not permit certification without analysis, since a certification with no reasoning attached cannot meet the Commission's stated purpose that "a reasonable investor could understand how the issuer made its determination." Specify three minimum elements: a privileged-function inventory of the admin keys, upgrade authorities, mint and freeze functions and equivalent capabilities, with the on-chain address or program identifier at which each resides and who controls each; where the issuer relies on governance dispersion, a governance-participation measurement with the methodology stated, including the address exclusions applied and whether delegated voting power was measured; and a related-person continuation statement on whether any related person is engaged in, or has undertaken to engage in, the efforts the issuer has ceased. Commissioner Peirce's 2021 Token Safe Harbor Proposal 2.0 asked more of its end-of-period exit report than proposed Rule 400 asks of Form TR.
  3. Extend the Rule 400(a) condition to affiliates, agents and privileged-function holders (proposed Rule 400(a); Questions 125 and 127). Proposed Rule 400 addresses only "the issuer," so an issuer may cease its essential managerial efforts while a founder-controlled foundation, an affiliate or a development company under common control continues them, and the crypto asset still exits the definition of "security" permanently. The 2026 Interpretation that Rule 400 is intended to codify already reads "issuer" to include affiliates and agents of the issuer or a promoter. Extend the condition to those persons and to any other person who controls a privileged function in the Form TR inventory, but not to every Rule 100 related person: not to a consultant, a family member, a validator or a code publisher who is neither an affiliate nor an agent and controls no privileged function. The release's existing carve-out for post-functionality maintenance, which applies "whether by the issuer or another party," keeps ordinary maintenance outside the condition.
  4. Specify the measurement methodology wherever holder or voting data is relied on (Questions 28, 130, 131 and 132). The same crypto asset can be characterized as dispersed or concentrated depending on choices no rule currently constrains. In the program's fifty-two-protocol audit, excluding burn addresses and protocol-controlled addresses lowered measured concentration by a median factor of 2.3 times across the thirty-two protocols with complete logging, while delegation raised effective voting concentration above post-exclusion holdings in thirteen of eighteen protocols with sufficient governance data (2.5 to 25.6 times, median 4.4 times), with five exceptions where a mature delegate program dispersed voting below holdings. Independent work by Matthias Nadler and Fabian Schär reaches the same conclusion about holder data. Specify, in the rule text or the adopting release, that any holder-concentration measure excludes burn and protocol-controlled addresses and, where governance runs through delegation, reflects delegated voting power rather than token holdings alone. The comment offers the audit's documented corrected methodology and its executable replication package as one reproducible procedure.
  5. Anchor the Rule 400(a) standard to network functionality (proposed Rule 400(a); Questions 131, 132 and 133). Rule 400(a) is scoped by the issuer's own representations, so an issuer that described an extensive development program must complete or abandon all of it, while one that described little has little to cease; that asymmetry runs against Rule 103's aim of fuller disclosure. Incorporate the functionality anchor the release already articulates as an independent element, so the condition turns on the state of the network as well as on the completion of what was promised, and define it in the Commission's terms rather than the issuer's, after the 2026 Interpretation's definition of a "functional" crypto system: one whose native crypto asset can be used on the system in accordance with its programmatic utility.
  6. Condition continued reliance on non-resumption, with prompt notice if efforts resume (proposed Rule 400, new paragraph (c); Questions 127 and 134). Rule 400 operates once, and its only forward-looking element is the statement that the issuer "does not intend to make" new representations or promises, which neither the Commission nor investors can observe. Condition continued reliance on the safe harbor on the issuer not resuming essential managerial efforts, and require prompt notice if it does, on the model of the Form 1-UC current report (the suggested rule text gives four business days). On Question 134, state that filing Form TR is not, by itself, an admission that the asset was subject to an investment contract; the filing remains subject to the antifraud provisions. The comment leaves open whether issuers will rely on the 2026 Interpretation instead of Rule 400, on which it has no evidence.
  7. State Rule 103(b)(1) progress against the milestones the issuer disclosed (proposed Rule 103(b)(1); Questions 26 through 30). Progress is measurable only against a stated baseline: if the initial disclosure describes undertakings in narrative terms without identifiable milestones, the Form TR completion analysis inherits the same indeterminacy. Require the issuer to identify the specific milestones or deliverables comprising its represented essential managerial efforts and to state progress against each. This is the one place the comment supports a prescriptive element, because the safe harbor later depends on it; otherwise it supports Rule 103's principles-based approach. The 2026 Interpretation already treats a business plan containing detailed milestones as the kind of representation that speaks directly to essential managerial efforts.
  8. Align the control test with sibling agencies' effective-control standards (adopting release; Questions 127 and 131). State in the adopting release that the Rule 400(a) determination looks to effective control rather than nominal arrangement, and that placing an effort with an affiliate or agent does not by itself establish cessation, the same diagnostic the program applied in its FinCEN (FINCEN-2026-0034, Recommendation 4) and FDIC (RIN 3064-AG29, Recommendation 5) comments. Divergent tests for who controls a network invite forum selection rather than compliance, and the Rule 400 line is becoming a boundary between two regimes: the CFTC's October 5, 2026 advance notice presents its framework as complementary to the safe harbor, so the Form TR record should rest on effective-control facts both agencies can read.

The problem identified

Proposed Rule 400 would deem a crypto asset no longer subject to an investment contract once the issuer has completed or permanently ceased the essential managerial efforts it represented or promised, is making and intends no new promises, and files a Form TR. Both operative facts are internal to the issuer: whether it has ceased its own efforts and whether it intends to make new promises are matters the issuer alone observes, while the Commission and investors observe the certification. The safe harbor reaches only "the issuer," although the 2026 Interpretation it would codify in part reads that term to include affiliates and agents; its scope shrinks with what the issuer promised; and it is one-way on its face. Question 131 asks whether there is "a more objective standard that we should use in place of the proposed standard in Rule 400(a)."

The program's evidence is that control is measurable, but in the present tense, from current state, not from what was represented at the offering. In the fifty-two-protocol live-governance audit published in Frontiers in Blockchain on August 5, 2026, launch insider allocation did not predict where governance concentration settles (Pearson r = 0.09, p = 0.55, N = 50), while current insider retention was informative (Spearman rho = 0.44, p = 0.005, N = 39; rho = 0.54 after a non-insider tautology check). A figure taken from the offering is a poor proxy for who controls the network at the Form TR date, and the current-state measures that do carry information are not required at all. The indicators most readily reached also misstate control, in both directions, unless the measurement methodology is specified.

A note on scope and evidence

The comment names no issuer or protocol. The audit measures launch allocation and insider retention against governance concentration; it does not measure offering-stage promises against later cessation of managerial efforts, and the comment draws from it only the limited inference stated above. Recommendation 1 also cites an on-chain admin-key and upgrade-authority audit of thirty-six protocols, in seventeen of which an upgrade authority could alter the core with no token-holder-governance-plus-timelock gate (one of them a borderline case, a permissioned thirteen-of-nineteen guardian set whose signers are independent rather than under common control). That count comes from a separate classification study that is unpublished, so the published article cannot be used to check it; the recommendation does not depend on it, and that sample is not the set of Rule 400 issuers.

The comment cites the House-passed text of H.R. 3633 for its drafting, not as a forecast of legislation, does not recommend audited or third-party-verified control assessments, and answers fourteen of the release's 154 numbered questions, leaving the rest for lack of program evidence. Every quotation in the comment was checked against an archived copy of its source, including the proposing release, the Federal Register text of the 2026 Interpretation and the House-passed bill text.
