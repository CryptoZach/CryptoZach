Comment to the OCC on the information collection in its GENIUS Act final rule: name each institution holding more than 5 percent of reserves in the monthly reserve report

Docket summary

The OCC's GENIUS Act final rule of October 23, 2026 carries its own information collection: the reporting, recordkeeping and disclosure requirements it places on the payment stablecoin issuers the OCC supervises. One of them is a monthly public report of reserve composition, which the rule's burden table estimates at 3,744 hours a year across 39 respondents. The rule asked for comments on the collection within 30 days of publication, a period that closed November 22, 2026, and the OCC submitted the collection to the Office of Management and Budget for review.

Where this letter fits

The third of the program's comments on the paperwork behind the OCC's GENIUS Act rules, filed the day after its comments on the application forms and the reporting forms, and after its comments on the OCC's proposed rule, with a supplement. The reporting forms comment asks the public quarterly report to name each institution holding more than 5 percent of reserves. This one asks the same of the monthly report the final rule requires.

The monthly reserve report in the OCC's final rule shows where each category of reserves is held by jurisdiction, not by institution, while the rule leaves custodied reserves outside its 40 percent counterparty limit, so holders cannot see the concentration the rule leaves to monitoring. The one recommendation would have the report's instructions name every institution holding more than 5 percent of reserve assets, in custody or on deposit, looking through money market funds to their custodians, with the amount held there.

Framework: Custody disclosure (what a holder can see of where reserves sit, and what the public report leaves out) Evidence: the OCC's final rule and its burden table, the companion reporting forms notice, and Federal Reserve Board staff analysis of the March 2023 stablecoin run

Recommendation

  1. Name each institution holding more than 5 percent of reserves. Have the instructions for the monthly reserve composition report ask each issuer to name every institution holding more than 5 percent of reserve assets, in custody or on deposit, looking through money market funds to their custodians, with the amount held there. Five percent is the rule's daily liquidity minimum, so a named institution would hold more than the entire minimum an issuer must keep on demand. Issuers already measure the amount at each institution, and the rule asks only for a format substantially similar to its template, so an institution-level row fits it.

The problem identified

The monthly report shows, for each category of reserve instruments, the amount and "the average tenor and geographic location of custody." It identifies a jurisdiction, not an institution. The final rule also excludes from its 40 percent counterparty limit the exposures that arise from reserve assets held by an appropriate custodian, while recognizing that institutions may be unable to return reserve assets, or slow to, and requiring issuers to manage that concentration. The custody concentration the limit no longer caps is therefore one the public report cannot show.

The report cannot show a deposit concentration either. In March 2023, holders of a major stablecoin learned of one at a bank, about 8 percent of reserves by Federal Reserve Board staff's account, only after the bank had failed. Issuers already know the amount at each institution, since the rule requires them to measure and manage that risk, and the OCC's revised weekly report would collect cash balances by depository institution.

A note on scope and evidence

The comment addresses one disclosure in the collection, and its practical utility to holders. It was sent on November 23, 2026, the first business day after the 30-day period the final rule set, while the collection was still under OMB review, and it asks to be considered to the extent practicable before OMB acts. The collection's reginfo.gov page offered no way to comment, so the comment went to the OCC by email, asking that it be forwarded to the OMB desk officer reviewing the collection. The March 2023 figure concerns a stablecoin that was not issued under the Act; it is offered to show what the report cannot reveal, not as a finding about any issuer.
