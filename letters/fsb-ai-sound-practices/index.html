FSB AI Sound Practices response: composition-chain accountability, measured in live agentic payments

Financial Stability Board consultation, "Sound Practices for Responsible Adoption of Artificial Intelligence (AI)" (published June 10, 2026) · Filed July 1, 2026 via the FSB secure online form · Responses due July 22, 2026 · Published by the FSB (no confidentiality requested)

Tokenization Systems' first submission to an international standard-setting body, and the first external deployment of its MVEP-AI and CLII-AI stack. A response to all seven questions of the FSB consultation on responsible AI adoption, anchored by the first control-layer measurement of live agentic payments: facilitator concentration, subsidy-driven volume, settlement finality, and composition-chain accountability across the x402 protocol on Base, Polygon, and Solana. The response adds what the report's institution-internal frame does not reach, a live market where autonomous agents already execute irreversible payments through an unsupervised operator layer, and offers the underlying dataset and reproduction code as supplementary material.

What the consultation asks

The FSB's June 2026 consultation report sets out twelve sound practices for the responsible adoption of AI by financial institutions, spanning governance, risk management, data, third-party dependencies, and monitoring, together with case studies and a glossary. It gives early and specific attention to agentic AI, and it invites additional case studies, particularly for nonbanks. The consultation poses seven questions, on the benefits and risks described, the comprehensiveness and clarity of the practices, the balance between all AI and emerging forms, flexibility over time, case studies for nonbanks, actionable insights, and the glossary. Responses were due July 22, 2026.

What Tokenization Systems contributes

The submission's value is empirical. In June 2026 Tokenization Systems completed what it understands to be the first control-layer measurement of live agentic payments, and the response uses it to name two risk areas the report's institution-internal frame does not reach, and to offer the nonbank case study the report invites.

  • Cross-operator composition. A live agentic payment is delivered by a chain of five operator classes (an agent runtime, an authorization protocol, a settlement facilitator, a token issuer, and a settlement network). Accountability degrades to the weakest operator in the chain, and the layer where the measured market concentrates, the facilitator, typically holds no contractual relationship with any financial institution. Institution-internal governance, however sound, does not reach this structure.
  • Integrity of AI-adoption statistics. Agents make transaction volume arbitrarily cheap to manufacture, so unweighted activity counts select for whichever operator has the strongest incentive to manufacture them. Adoption indicators built on raw activity counts in agentic markets will systematically mismeasure adoption.

The measured case study (May 2026, three chains). Settlement-facilitator concentration is above the highly-concentrated threshold on every reading (payment-count HHI 6,009 on Base, 4,203 on Solana; corrected-volume HHI 4,040 and 4,915; effective single-operator service on Polygon). The count-dominant and value-dominant operators differ on two separate chains. The largest single count contributor is a chain operator's official facilitator under a public gas-rebate program, which produced 5.04 million May payments, 56.8 percent of cross-chain counts, from a fleet of 35 buyer wallets. Measured at the value layer the market is currently small: corrected cross-chain May volume is $940,323 across 8.87 million payments, 0.0000376 percent of USDC transfer value on the same rails. Settlement is irreversible at the protocol layer, and none of the top 25 sellers by dollar volume resolves to a named operator from public records. No impropriety is implied by any figure; the measured subsidy program is public, and the claims are about market structure, not conduct.

Requested edits

Five edits, each compatible with the report's menu approach and proportionality principle. They clarify how the existing menu applies when AI systems can execute transactions; they do not ask the FSB to convert the report into a prescriptive standard.

  1. Composition-chain documentation. For AI use cases with execution authority, document the full operator chain through settlement, including non-contractual settlement layers, and record who can halt, reverse, or remediate a transaction at each layer. (Sound Practices 3 and 12)
  2. Execution-authority evidence package. Name a minimum evidence package before any AI system receives authority to move funds or bind the institution, including a reconstructible authorization-context record, not merely a settlement record. (Sound Practices 5 and 10)
  3. System-level dependency monitoring. Acknowledge measured system-level concentration of shared AI-payment dependencies as a complement to institution-level registers, which cannot observe market-wide facilitator concentration. (Sound Practice 12)
  4. Agentic-AI glossary distinction. Distinguish agentic AI generally from agentic AI with execution authority; advisory autonomy and irreversible transaction authority differ in kind. (Glossary and Sound Practice 10)
  5. Supply-chain scope. Reconsider the service-relationship limit in "supply chain," or add "composition chain" as a companion term that reaches operators outside direct service relationships. (Glossary, Sound Practices 3 and 12)
