Comment to the FDIC on merger transactions: keep a merger with a payment stablecoin issuer subsidiary out of rapid approval until its stablecoins are redeemed

Docket summary

The FDIC's proposed rule of September 22, 2026 would revise how it reviews bank mergers under the Bank Merger Act. It would create a category of de minimis merger transactions that includes a corporate reorganization in which a bank takes in its own operating subsidiary, and an acquisition small enough to fall under a size threshold. A de minimis reorganization would be deemed approved five business days after a substantially complete letter filing unless the Attorney General objects, and the FDIC could not remove it from rapid processing. No de minimis transaction would have a public comment period. One of the proposal's questions asks whether the rule should expressly address mergers involving a subsidiary that issues payment stablecoins under the GENIUS Act. Comments were due November 23, 2026.

Where this letter fits

The program has commented on the FDIC's three GENIUS Act proposals: the approval of payment stablecoin issuance by subsidiaries of FDIC-supervised banks, the requirements and standards for the issuers the FDIC supervises, and their anti-money laundering and sanctions compliance. This comment carries those proposals into the merger rule, at the point where a merger moves, combines or winds up a bank's payment stablecoin subsidiary. It takes no position on the proposal's other reforms.

As proposed, a merger that folds a payment stablecoin subsidiary into its parent bank could qualify as de minimis and be approved on a short letter filing that never asks whether any of the subsidiary's payment stablecoins are still outstanding, though the GENIUS Act places the issuer's obligations and its holders' protections in the subsidiary and does not let the parent bank issue payment stablecoins itself. The five recommendations would keep such a merger out of the de minimis category until every outstanding payment stablecoin has been redeemed, put the stablecoin facts in merger filings, weigh the GENIUS Act approval factors when a merger makes the FDIC an issuer's regulator, write the enterprise-wide anti-money laundering standard into the rule, and measure deposits the same way whatever ledger records them.

Framework: Statutory continuity (where the GENIUS Act places an issuer's obligations and its holders' protections, and what a merger does to them) Evidence: the FDIC's proposal, the GENIUS Act, the FDIC's three GENIUS Act proposals, and an OCC conditional approval of December 2025, cited as an analogy

Recommendations

  1. Keep a merger with a payment stablecoin issuer subsidiary out of the de minimis category until its stablecoins are redeemed. Add to the de minimis definition a condition that no payment stablecoin issued by an issuer subsidiary the transaction merges with or acquires remains outstanding on the date of the letter filing, so that it governs both the reorganization route and the size route. The parent bank is not a permitted issuer, and a subsidiary merged into it while its payment stablecoins are outstanding would carry the redemption obligation into an institution the GENIUS Act does not permit to issue them. A wind-up filed before redemption is complete would generally remain eligible for the expedited processing the proposal gives corporate reorganizations, where the FDIC keeps its ordinary review.
  2. Ask for the payment stablecoin facts in every merger filing that involves an issuer. Add to the letter filing a certification that no payment stablecoin of an issuer subsidiary being acquired remains outstanding, with the date the last was redeemed. Add to other merger filings a short schedule for each issuer subsidiary: its outstanding payment stablecoins and reserve composition as most recently published, the share of its reserves held at the resulting bank or its affiliates, its place in the resulting organization, its federal payment stablecoin regulator before and after the merger, and, for a subsidiary to be wound down, the redemption plan, how holders will be told, and the timetable. Most of these facts the issuer already publishes monthly.
  3. Weigh the GENIUS Act approval factors when a merger moves an issuer to FDIC supervision. The GENIUS Act ties a subsidiary issuer's regulator to its parent bank's, so the merger of a national bank or a state member bank into a state nonmember bank can make the FDIC the regulator of an issuer it never approved. In that case the FDIC's review of financial and managerial resources should include the factors the Act sets for approving an issuer: its ability, on its financial condition and resources, to meet the Act's requirements; the bar on officers and directors convicted of the listed felonies; the competence, experience and integrity of its management; and its redemption policy. The Act's rule that issuing on an open, public or decentralized network is not a ground for denial should apply too, and one merger filing should satisfy any notice the same change of parent would otherwise require.
  4. Write the enterprise-wide anti-money laundering standard into the rule, and include any issuer subsidiary in it. The proposal's preamble expects each bank, including the resulting bank, to have a Bank Secrecy Act and anti-money laundering program commensurate with the volume and risk of its enterprise-wide business model, but the proposed rule text only restates the statute. The final rule should codify that standard and say that the enterprise includes any payment stablecoin issuer subsidiary, which carries its own Bank Secrecy Act duties and must be able to block, freeze and reject transactions and to comply with lawful orders.
  5. Measure deposits the same way whatever ledger records them, and do not count payment stablecoin balances as deposits. The GENIUS Act treats a deposit recorded on a distributed ledger as a deposit, not a payment stablecoin. The final rule should confirm that such a deposit counts in the initial competition screen, and is attributed to a branch or a central office, on the same basis as any other deposit, and that payment stablecoin balances are not counted as deposits in any adjustment for nonbank competitors, since reserves held as bank deposits are already counted once.

The problem identified

The proposal's own question names the case: a bank with a payment stablecoin subsidiary may exit that business and wind the subsidiary up by merging it into the bank. As proposed, that merger can be de minimis, as a corporate reorganization that leaves the bank's legal and financial risk substantially identical, or, for a small subsidiary, by size alone. The GENIUS Act puts the issuer's obligations in the subsidiary: it must hold identifiable reserves at least one to one against its outstanding payment stablecoins, and in its insolvency the holders' claims on the required reserves have priority. The Act does not say how either protection applies once the issuer has merged into its parent; it does not use the words merger, successor or change in control. Before the merger, the bank is exposed to the subsidiary as its owner. After it, the bank would owe the redemption of every payment stablecoin still outstanding. That is not substantially identical risk.

The proposal already reasons this way about another liability: a reorganization would not be de minimis if the operating subsidiary were in substantial, ongoing litigation the bank was not already exposed to. Outstanding payment stablecoins are a liability of the same kind, and one with a bright line. Once every one has been redeemed, the roll-up is the routine simplification the category was written for. The FDIC's own GENIUS Act standards proposal supplies the starting event, since it requires an issuer that decides to redeem all its outstanding payment stablecoins to notify the FDIC.

A note on scope and evidence

The comment answers the proposal's question on payment stablecoin issuer subsidiaries and the questions that answer depends on: the roll-up category of de minimis transactions, the content of merger filings, the financial and managerial resources factor, the anti-money laundering factor, and the deposit measure in the initial competition screen. Three of the recommendations carry suggested rule text. The OCC approval it cites, of December 2025, was not a Bank Merger Act decision; it is cited only as an analogy, an approval by a federal banking agency that included a merger and made GENIUS Act compliance one of its conditions. The comment went to the FDIC by email on the last day of the comment period.
