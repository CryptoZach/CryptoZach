Treasury GENIUS Act section 3 comment: confirm on-chain that a foreign issuer's lawful-order capability exists, then reach the controls around it

Docket summary

Proposed 12 CFR part 1523 implements section 3 of the GENIUS Act: who may issue payment stablecoins in the United States, and when digital asset service providers may offer or sell them to persons in the United States, including the stablecoins of foreign issuers that meet the section 18 conditions. Proposed Sec. 1523.3(c) governs reliance on a foreign issuer's representation about lawful orders, and proposed Sec. 1523.2(c) and Sec. 1523.3(e) take a reasonable-belief approach to issuance and to offers and sales that Treasury asks whether to replace with stricter alternatives. The notice was published August 18, 2026 (91 Fed. Reg. 53368), and its comment period closes October 19, 2026.

Macro context

The program's second comment to Treasury, after its April 2026 comment on the principles for the GENIUS Act state-similarity assessment (TREAS-DO-2026-0232). It carries forward two positions from the program's FinCEN comments: that a list-comparison obligation is only as operative as the list and its notice mechanism, and that the account-based framing of the lawful-order obligation must be reconciled with wallet-based stablecoin transfers. Treasury was already engaged, so the filing adds a docket and no agency.

Of the two facts proposed Sec. 1523.3(c) turns on, whether a foreign payment stablecoin issuer has the technological capability to comply with a lawful order is readable from its deployed contract and its on-chain history, while whether it will comply in the future is not observable at all. Treasury has correctly declined to require certainty about the second. The comment asks Treasury to take the corresponding step on the first: name an on-chain check that the capability exists as a floor of reasonable due diligence, then carry that diligence to the controls around the capability, a gap the comment states as a limitation of the program's own index.

Recommendations

  1. On-chain capability check as a diligence floor (proposed Sec. 1523.3(c); Question 52). State, in the rule text or the adopting release, that reasonable due diligence on a foreign issuer's representation includes confirming from the deployed contract and its on-chain history that the represented capability to comply with a lawful order exists and that the authority to invoke it is identifiable, where the payment stablecoin is issued on a public ledger. A freeze or burn function either exists in the deployed code or it does not, and prior invocations are a matter of public record, so the check needs no cooperation from the issuer: an examination of the ledger, not an audit.
  2. Diligence must reach the controls, not just the capability (proposed Sec. 1523.3(c); Question 52). Extend the expected diligence to who may invoke the function and under what internal authorization, whether limits exist on the privileged operations the same key can perform, and whether the issuer publishes a response commitment for a lawful order. A documented production overmint at a highly regulated issuer, whose freeze and mint capability scored high on the program's own index while a supply cap, a per-transaction limit and multisignature minting were absent, shows that a check of the capability alone would have cleared it; the comment states this as a limitation of the program's own instrument. The Act already pairs the capability with policies and procedures for permitted issuers.
  3. Replace the unobservable "will comply" with an observable proxy (proposed Sec. 1523.3(c) and section 3(b)(2); Questions 52 and 53). Confirm that the forward-looking limb is satisfied by the issuer's documented history of responding to lawful orders and a published response commitment, plus a duty to re-check on defined triggers (a change in control arrangements or in the contract's privileged functions, a section 8 designation of an affiliated issuer, or a failure to respond to a known lawful order), with records retained; and retain the proposed "knows, has reason to know, or should know" standard over the no-reason-to-suspect alternative.
  4. Key the mandatory section 8 check to a published determination (proposed Sec. 1523.3(c); section 8, 12 U.S.C. 5907; Question 52). Under section 8(b) the trading prohibition takes effect 30 days after its Federal Register notification, so a check limited to a prohibition in effect can pass while the published record already shows noncompliance. Confirm that the mandatory element covers a published determination of noncompliance not since lifted, and state the designation list's current state, where a provider checks it, and whether Treasury will maintain a machine-readable source.
  5. Retain reasonable belief, and name the observable evidence for it (proposed Sec. 1523.2(c) and Sec. 1523.3(e); Questions 38, 44, 62 and 64). Keep the reasonable-belief approach over the per se alternatives for both issuance and offers or sales, and name the categories of evidence that support it: deployed geographic-blocking controls and their coverage, sanctions and jurisdictional screening at onboarding, controls against location spoofing, and the handling of indeterminate results. The controls that establish location are themselves observable, so the standard loses nothing on verifiability.
  6. Assess the capability where the invoking authority resides (proposed Sec. 1523.3(c) and Sec. 1523.1; Question 52, see also Question 24). Confirm that the inquiry reaches the party that actually holds the authority to invoke the compliance function, including an affiliate, a foundation, a contract administrator or a multi-party arrangement, because a representation signed by an issuer whose key is held elsewhere describes a capability the signer does not control. No new obligation arises in the ordinary case in which the issuer holds its own keys.
  7. Align the lawful-order test across the three rulemakings that carry it (proposed Sec. 1523.3(c) and Sec. 1523.1(c); Question 4). State in the final rule's preamble how this capability standard relates to the lawful-order obligations in the FinCEN permitted payment stablecoin issuer rulemakings: whether an issuer that satisfies one satisfies the others, and whether a provider may rely on a determination made under them. On Question 4, align the definition of lawful order with FinCEN's, or state where and why the two differ.

The problem identified

Section 3(b)(2) of the GENIUS Act lets a digital asset service provider offer a foreign issuer's payment stablecoin only where the issuer has the technological capability to comply, and will comply, with lawful orders. Proposed Sec. 1523.3(c) lets the provider rely on the issuer's representation to that effect, provided it conducts reasonable due diligence regarding the representation, and Treasury has left what that diligence requires open by design, posing the question directly in Question 52.

The two facts the representation covers are not equally observable. A freeze, burn or blacklist function either exists in a deployed contract or it does not, the authority to invoke it resides at an identifiable address, and prior invocations are public record, so the capability limb can be checked from outside the issuer without its cooperation. Whether the issuer will comply in the future cannot be observed at all. Left unstated, the diligence standard leaves the checkable limb to the issuer's assertion and the uncheckable one unfalsifiable in both directions.

A note on scope and evidence

The comment names no issuer. The production incident behind Recommendation 2 is drawn from the program's own analysis of a documented October 2025 overmint and is offered as a limitation of the program's own index, not as a finding about any operator. Every quotation in the comment was checked against an archived copy of its source, including the published Federal Register text and the government text of the Act.
