Decision lens

Infrastructure accountability, not slogans

Built for teams that need to name the operator or interface that owns failure handling, require artifacts that survive committee review, and treat concentration and routing defaults as first-class policy facts. CLII scores regulatory intensity at the gateway layer. MVEP supplies pass or fail gates before scale. Together they give a reproducible way to discuss where monitoring should attach and when redesign or suspension is warranted, with the same logic carrying from stablecoin gateways to AI deployment and operator incentives.

The research program sits at the intersection of programmable money, tokenization, and institutional design. It pairs empirical gateway and stress evidence with frameworks that supervisors, risk committees, and product leaders can use in packets.

  • How do you prove an AI or infrastructure accountability story with named artifacts and tests, not narrative alone?

Routing the Dollar

What it is: A data-driven study of 19 gateway entities and 51 wallet addresses showing that the same dollar token inherits different regulatory obligations, different Fed-policy sensitivity, and different crisis behavior depending on who routes it. Introduces the CLII, which measures how heavily regulated each gateway is across five dimensions.

Why it matters: Moves analysis from token labels to operators, reserve chains, and routing networks, with named metrics for concentration and stress behavior.

Who it's for: Policy, central-bank, market-structure, and infrastructure readers building transferable analogies to AI routing and operator accountability.

Prepared for the FRBNY Dollar Conference.

Auditing Governance Concentration Beyond Token Allocation

What it is: Cross-sectional analysis of 52 protocols spanning DePIN (Decentralized Physical Infrastructure Networks, where token incentives coordinate real-world hardware deployment), DeFi (decentralized finance), L1/L2 (Layer 1 / Layer 2 blockchain infrastructure), and social tokens. Allocation design does not predict governance concentration (Pearson r = 0.09, p = 0.55, N = 50); sector membership does (Mann-Whitney p = 0.028, Cohen's d = 0.65). Five-lens institutional-design framework (Kantian publicity, Rawlsian legitimacy, Pettit non-domination, Ostromian polycentricity, Hayekian knowledge-use) and a five-class exclusion typology removing 133 protocol-controlled addresses across 38 protocols plus 64 Nansen-labeled exchange-custody wallets across 21.

Why it matters: Gives the program a criteria layer for evaluating protocol governance alongside simulation results, connecting token and infrastructure design to standards of institutional legitimacy that AI governance work also needs. Lead-finding framing is "allocation null plus four supporting findings", not "no covariate predicts concentration."

Who it's for: Governance designers, DAO researchers, protocol teams, and AI governance readers who need explicit fairness and accountability criteria.

Published at Frontiers in Blockchain on August 5, 2026 (Blockchain Economics). Frontiers in Blockchain 9:1853465, doi 10.3389/fbloc.2026.1853465. Also on SSRN 6599278.

The Control Layer of Agentic Payments

What it is: The first control-layer measurement of live agentic payments: registry-merged facilitator attribution (138 addresses across 29 facilitators) and settlement-fingerprint queries across Base, Polygon, and Solana (x402). Every concentration reading exceeds the antitrust highly-concentrated threshold, and buyer cardinality separates organic adoption from subsidized and proxy pipelines without any wash-trading heuristic.

Why it matters: The composition chain that delivers an agent-initiated payment currently has no layer at which accountability composes, a gap neither AI-governance frameworks nor payments regulation sees. This memo anchors the FSB AI Sound Practices consultation response.

Who it's for: Financial-stability authorities, payments regulators, and AI-governance teams that need agent-economy claims grounded in measured settlement structure.

Research memo, June 2026; reproducible from committed SQL. Anchors the FSB response (filed July 1, 2026).

Who Burns the Tokens?

What it is: A within-sector GeoDePIN study across 13 protocols, anchored by a 34-month Helium case: Subsidy-to-Revenue trajectory, burn-mint parity, and demand-concentration measurability by payment architecture. Uses the Governance Concentration exclusion methodology and Ostromian polycentricity framing; the August 2025 emission halving and HIP-147 (Helium Improvement Proposal 147; the coverage-to-service reward reform) in Helium’s fiscal path.

Why it matters: Pairs the cross-sectional protocol governance in Governance Concentration with a vertical, operator-scale story about who pays, how fast sustainability arrives, and when the payer base is even visible on-chain.

Who it’s for: DePIN, protocol, and tokenomics readers who need governance criteria plus measured fiscal and burn-side mechanics.

SSRN 6483619. Submission to Blockchain: Research and Applications (Elsevier) pending.
