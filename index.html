The decision map

Decide what this actually is.

Whatever shape the product takes, the decision map has already planned for it. Each step asks one question and opens one door; the field guide walks the sequence in full.

  1. Classify. Which object is this, before anyone scores it? The dollar-product and infrastructure maps name the class, and the class decides which rulebook applies.
  2. Trace. Can the same dollar behave differently depending on how you hold it? Follow the token to the asset, reserves, institutions, and infrastructure underneath it.
  3. Map. Who can actually say no? Identify operators, gateways, counterparties, and control points.
  4. Compare. Does owning the token give you what you think you are buying? Test the tokenized product against the rights and behavior of the underlying asset.
  5. Stress. What changes when the happy path stops? Ask what changes when a critical layer fails or becomes constrained.
  6. Decide. Are the remaining differences acceptable for the intended use? That call belongs to the institution taking the risk. Name the step you are on.

The same token behaves differently across gateways. Legal and operational equivalence must be proven under stress. Technical decentralization relocates intervention authority to the control layer. Every step in the map tests one of those three realities.

Agent infrastructure · Control-layer extension

A warning advises an agent. A hard gate stops it.

The same control-layer principle that governs digital dollars and tokenized securities applies to AI agent fleets: a token label or a prompt rule states what should happen, while the operating layer decides what can actually execute. We built a coordination layer that lets dozens of AI research and coding agents work on one shared codebase at the same time: each agent runs in its own isolated workspace, every shared result records which session produced it and what evidence it used, and every safeguard is graded by whether agent behavior changes after it fires.

13,098 / 13,156
Unsafe actions stopped at the gate

Enforced before execution. When a destructive or invalid command can be identified in advance, a hard gate blocks it outright rather than only printing a warning. Across 13,156 measured attempts that reached one of these gates on both of the fleet's machines, through 23 October 2026, the system stopped the action in 13,098 cases. In the other 58, the agent used the documented override and continued (holding in about 199 of every 200 attempts).

About Tokenization Systems

An independent research, advisory, and infrastructure practice for digital financial systems: stablecoins, tokenized deposits, the settlement rails that move value between institutions, and token-economy design. The work holds itself to the standard it applies to the systems it studies: claims trace to sources, frameworks are tested against adversarial conditions, and boundaries are named explicitly.

Founder Zach Zukowski. Six years as Senior Investment Analyst and founding research hire at Borderless Capital, a Web3-focused registered investment adviser: about 250 investments backed, about $500M deployed, and 100 portfolio companies advised, as of 2026.

Frameworks Control Layer Intensity Index (CLII), a 0-to-1 score for how heavily regulated a gateway is, where a gateway is any issuer or distributor routing dollars between on-chain and off-chain rails. Minimum Viable Equivalence Pack (MVEP), a ten-category diligence standard for tokenized money and securities, run by the institution taking the risk rather than paid for by the issuer. MVEP is the entry gate, checked once before you commit; CLII is the ongoing check that flags when a product drifts away from what it cleared on.

Program Nine working papers on SSRN and 25 federal comment letters across the GENIUS Act and related rulemaking dockets, organized in two tracks: Track A (stablecoins, gateways, and settlement) and Track B (tokenomics; DePIN, meaning decentralized physical infrastructure networks; and protocol-network governance).

Patent-pending systems 42 filed USPTO applications across 32 families, most of them infrastructure for running many AI agents on one shared codebase at the same time. Read the full list, grouped by the problem each filing addresses.
