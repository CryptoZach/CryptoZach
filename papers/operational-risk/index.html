Operational Risk in Token Economies: How Infrastructure Failures Strengthen Adaptive Controllers and Break Reputation Systems

~15 min read · ~6,500 words · 2026 · SSRN

Description current as of March 2026.

Infrastructure failures change token-economy behavior in ways economic-only simulations miss. This paper extends a DePIN token simulation with an operational-risk layer calibrated from Ethereum, Helium, GEODNET, and Filecoin, using the emerging ValOS (Validator Operating System) and NORS (Node Operator Risk Standard, developed by Alluvial and collaborators) standards as the taxonomy for what can go wrong in production. The result is a split finding: temporary infrastructure failures make adaptive emission control more resilient under stress, while a threshold-gated reputation system can become structurally nonfunctional under routine operational risk.

1

Under the tested parameterization, operational failures strengthen PID emission control. When temporary outages are misread as permanent departures, the controller overcompensates with higher rewards, building a financial buffer before the next economic shock. Under competitor shock, variance compression improves from 6.25x to 6.89x once operational risk is added. The result is counterintuitive and parameterization-dependent: at higher failure intensity, the buffer can erode.

2

The reputation system does not gradually drift; it collapses. Model parameters: uptime threshold (0.99), season length (90 days). The recovery threshold is the proportion of pre-shock reputation an operator must regain before re-entering full rotation. At the default 99% uptime threshold over a 90-day season, average reputation decays to 0.18 on a 0-to-5 scale by year 5 of simulation clock (not calendar time), effectively disabling reputation-weighted governance. The fix is the recovery threshold: lowering it from 0.99 to 0.95 restores average reputation to approximately 1.0 by simulation year 5; 0.90 restores it to approximately 2.0. Changing the decay rate alone does not restore stability at the 0.99 threshold.

3

The paper is as much a methods contribution as a findings paper. It maps 74 active ValOS risk vectors and 64 testable controls, plus 31 NORS control objectives, into seven simulatable parameters and stress-tests the result across a 624-configuration battery. That bridge from operator-risk standards to calibrated simulation is the reusable contribution.

What problem it solves

Most token simulations assume operators fail only for incentive reasons. This paper asks what happens when the infrastructure itself fails: outages, key loss, software bugs, correlated incidents, or regulatory disruption affecting operators who are still economically viable. Its contribution is a replicable process for extending token simulation with empirically calibrated operational risk, using ValOS and NORS as the taxonomic bridge from production failures to simulation parameters.

What this paper changes

Most token simulations model why operators leave for economic reasons (price decline, insufficient rewards, competitive alternatives). This paper asks what happens when operators are still economically viable but suffer real production failures: outages, key loss, software bugs, correlated infrastructure incidents, or regulatory disruption.

The methodological contribution is a replicable process for extending token simulation with empirically calibrated operational risk. The paper maps 74 active ValOS risk vectors and 64 testable controls, plus 31 NORS certification control objectives, into seven simulatable parameters. That mapping is the institutional bridge between operator-risk standards and token-economy simulation: it translates what can go wrong in production into parameters a model can test.

The paper also refines the earlier adaptive-tokenomics result. The baseline MeshNet model showed PID-based variance reduction under competitor shock; the operational-risk extension shows the gap versus static emission widens under stress:

  • Competitor shock: Static variability rises from 0.544 to 0.569; PID falls from 0.087 to 0.082.
  • Regulatory shock: Static rises from 0.229 to 0.561; PID falls from 0.093 to 0.070.
  • Bull conditions: Outcomes are effectively identical.

The beneficial effect is parameterization-dependent. At higher operational-risk levels or higher failure correlation, the buffer can erode and may eventually overshoot into the dilution loop identified in the earlier MeshNet paper.

Core findings

1. Temporary outages can make adaptive emission more stabilizing

The controller mistakes recoverable downtime for economic exit, emits extra rewards, and unintentionally pre-loads operator income before the major shock arrives. That explains why the operational-risk version performs better than the economic-only version in the tested stress cases.

The paper is explicit that this is parameterization-dependent. The tested calibration (0.5% daily downtime probability, 10% correlated-failure exposure per event, 3-day mean recovery) produces the beneficial buffer. Higher intensity or higher correlation reduces the advantage, even if it does not reverse it within the tested range.

2. Correlated failures are sharp but transient at the tested settings

The biggest single-day node-count drop from a correlated infrastructure failure is 24.94%, but it remains within the controller's operating range and recovers within days. The operational-risk story is one of temporary disruption with asymmetric downstream effects on different mechanisms, not persistent infrastructure collapse under the base calibration.

3. Reputation is the true fragility point

With a daily downtime probability of 0.5%, the probability of zero downtime over a 90-day season is approximately 0.64. Roughly 36% of operators lose recovery eligibility in any given season. Over four seasons, only about 16% retain perfect eligibility. Once a season is missed, the 15% seasonal decay compounds without offset.

The paper treats this as structural collapse: the uptime threshold is set above the operational floor the operator base can actually achieve. Average reputation decays to 0.18 on a 0-to-5 scale by year 5, effectively disabling reputation-weighted governance.

The fix is specific: lower the recovery threshold. At 0.95, average reputation restores to approximately 1.0 by year 5. At 0.90, it restores to approximately 2.0. Changing the decay parameter alone does not restore stability at the 0.99 threshold. This is one of the most actionable findings in the paper.

4. The methodological bridge matters as much as the findings

ValOS and NORS are not background context; they are the institutional bridge between simulation and production controls. ValOS supplies the granular taxonomy of risk vectors and controls; NORS supplies the certification logic institutions can audit. The case-study signal is that this control language is already becoming economically consequential: Lido V3 stVaults launched on Ethereum mainnet on January 30, 2026 and use operator risk scores to set collateral requirements; Figment achieved Full NORS Certification for Ethereum on February 4, 2026, evidence that these standards are moving from voluntary checklists toward enforceable operating rules.

Methods / Data

The simulation battery comprises approximately 624 runs:

  • 240 baseline runs (from the Adaptive Tokenomics paper)
  • 240 operational-risk ensemble runs (30 seeds x 8 configurations with the operational-risk layer active)
  • 84 per-parameter sweeps (sensitivity across individual operational-risk parameters)
  • 12 protocol-specific runs (calibrated to Ethereum, Helium, GEODNET, and Filecoin failure profiles)
  • 40 correlated-shock runs (testing simultaneous infrastructure failures across operator groups)
  • 8 interaction tests (examining how operational risk interacts with economic shocks)

Calibration: Ethereum validator performance data, Helium network contraction and hotspot reliability, GEODNET station uptime, and Filecoin sector fault rates. MeshNet base assumptions: 0.5% daily downtime probability, 3% annual exit rate, 0.05% annual operational slashing rate, 10% correlated-failure exposure per event, 20% partial-failure share, mean recovery time of 3 days capped at 42 days.

The paper notes that these point estimates reflect author judgment within documented ranges; they are not mechanical aggregation from a single source.

Limitations

The paper is explicit about the following boundaries:

  • The beneficial overreaction result is parameterization-dependent. Higher correlated-failure intensity reduces the PID advantage, even if it does not reverse it within the tested range.
  • Most per-parameter sweeps are single-seed; multi-seed confirmation is complete only for the 240-run ensemble configurations.
  • Operational risk is assigned at infrastructure-group level. Operator-level heterogeneity (whether smaller operators face systematically higher risk) is explicitly identified as future work.
  • Cross-chain operational-risk correlation is not modeled; each chain's failure profile is tested independently.
  • Welfare analysis (whether the operational-risk buffer is socially efficient or just stabilizing) remains an open extension.
