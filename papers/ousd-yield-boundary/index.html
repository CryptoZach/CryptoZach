Open USD and the Yield Boundary: Distributor Revenue-Share Under the GENIUS Act

Briefing · Stablecoin Regulation · GENIUS Act · Dollar Infrastructure

Briefing version 1, published July 2, 2026, sixteen days before the GENIUS Act final implementing rules are due. A post-rule addendum will score the four-move section against the actual rule text.

On June 30, 2026, Open Standard, a consortium of more than 140 companies including Visa, Mastercard, Stripe, BlackRock, Coinbase, and BNY, announced Open USD (OUSD): a dollar stablecoin with zero minting and redemption fees whose reserve revenue, minus a management fee, is returned to the partners who adopt and distribute it. Per every source reviewed, that revenue flows to partner businesses, not to end holders. The GENIUS Act prohibits stablecoin issuers from paying holders interest or yield, and distributor revenue-share is the nearest legal-design neighbor to holder yield that the prohibition does not facially reach. This briefing maps where the announced design sits relative to the statute, the two pending implementing proposals, and the pending legislative amendment, and states four moves the final rules, due July 18, 2026, could make, each with its consequence, so the analysis can be scored against the actual rule text when it lands.

At a glance

Question
Does the announced OUSD design, where reserve revenue is shared with distribution partners rather than paid to holders, sit inside or outside the GENIUS Act's yield prohibition?

Answer
As announced, outside every text currently on the table. The statute reaches issuer-to-holder payments; the OCC's proposed presumption requires a holder-facing second hop that OUSD lacks; the FDIC's broader formulation still runs through the issuer-pays-yield characterization; and the pending CLARITY amendment prohibits distributor-to-customer payments, not issuer-to-distributor shares. The design's viability turns on two unresolved parameters: whether the revenue share is calibrated to partner-held balances, and who counts as the holder of custodied tokens. Both are contested; neither is settled by any current text.

Evidence base
The enacted GENIUS Act text; the OCC's proposed implementing rule (91 Fed. Reg. 10199, March 2, 2026) and the FDIC's parallel proposal (91 Fed. Reg. 18534, April 10, 2026); the Senate-stage CLARITY Act Section 404 print; the Senate floor record on the Warren and Hickenlooper amendments; Circle's S-1 distribution-economics disclosures; the Council of Economic Advisers' April 2026 white paper on the yield prohibition; and contemporaneous public reporting on the June 30 announcement.

Four findings

1

The announced design occupies the one cell every current text leaves open

Decompose the yield path into two hops: issuer to distributor (hop one), distributor to holder (hop two). The statute reaches issuer-to-holder payments. The OCC's proposed presumption requires both hops. The pending CLARITY amendment reaches hop two only. Announced OUSD is hop one alone: a business revenue-share with no holder-facing payment. The Senate considered extending the prohibition to distributors and declined.

What this means: A consortium can route reserve economics to its distribution layer without facially triggering the yield prohibition as written, as proposed by either agency, or as amended by the pending bill.

2

Two undisclosed or unsettled parameters decide whether that holds

First, the calibration basis: no source discloses whether a partner's share is proportional to the OUSD balances it holds or custodies, to transaction volume, or to a flat participation tier. Second, the holder definition: a published custody-law argument holds that an exchange, not its customer, is the holder of tokens in its omnibus wallets, in which case a balance-proportional share to a custodying partner is literally a payment to a holder measured by its holdings. The statutory word "solely" then cuts both ways: conditioning payment on distribution activity defeats a narrow reading and invites an anti-evasion reading.

What this means: Watch the consortium agreements and the final rules' definitions, not the marketing.

3

The incumbent arrangement is more exposed than the challenger's

The closest live analog is the Circle and Coinbase USDC arrangement: Circle paid Coinbase $907.9 million in distribution fees in 2024, and Coinbase pays advertised rewards to customers holding USDC on its platform. That is a two-hop structure with a holder-facing payment, the shape the pending rule texts actually target. Announced OUSD is one hop shorter. The same firm sits on both sides: Coinbase is simultaneously Circle's USDC distribution counterparty and an OUSD launch partner.

What this means: A rule that squeezes holder-facing rewards while leaving business revenue-share open would push distribution economics from the rewards model toward the consortium model.

4

The final rules have four plausible moves; the design survives three of them

Finalize the OCC presumption as proposed (OUSD stays outside it); broaden the trigger to balance-calibrated distributor compensation (reaches OUSD only on one calibration, and invites statutory-authority challenge on the Senate amendment record); codify a holder definition (decides the custody question in either direction); or track the statute and refer the gap to Congress (undisturbed). Each move has a stated consequence in the body below.

What Open Standard announced

Four design facts matter. First, zero minting and redemption fees, with no volume limits: the issuer-side fee surface is set to zero. Second, reserve revenue routed to distribution: most revenue generated from reserves, minus a management fee, is returned to participants who adopt and distribute the token, and the payees are the consortium's partner businesses, not end holders. Third, consortium governance: Open Standard describes itself as an independent company whose board is drawn from partner businesses. Fourth, the regulated issuer of record is undisclosed: Open Standard states reserves will be held at major financial institutions in compliance with US regulatory requirements, but no source discloses which entity will hold permitted payment stablecoin issuer status, under what charter or license, or under which supervisor. Launch is stated for later in 2026, which lands near the GENIUS Act's effective date (eighteen months after enactment, January 2027, or 120 days after final rules, whichever is earlier).

One further design parameter is undisclosed and load-bearing: the calibration basis of the revenue share. Whether a partner's share is proportional to the OUSD balances it holds or custodies, to the transaction volume it originates, to a flat participation tier, or to some blend is stated nowhere in the reviewed sources. Most of the legal analysis below pivots on this parameter.

The four texts that draw the boundary

The statute. GENIUS Act Section 4(a)(11) provides that no permitted payment stablecoin issuer or foreign payment stablecoin issuer shall pay the holder of any payment stablecoin any form of interest or yield (whether in cash, tokens, or other consideration) solely in connection with the holding, use, or retention of such payment stablecoin. Three elements do the work: the regulated payor is the issuer; the protected payee class is holders; and the payment must be solely in connection with holding, use, or retention. The legislative history is direct: during Senate consideration, the Warren and Hickenlooper amendments would have extended the prohibition to third parties such as affiliates, distributors, and merchants; both were considered and neither was adopted. The Council of Economic Advisers' April 2026 white paper states the resulting gap plainly: the prohibition may not fully bind, because the statutory language prohibits payments by the issuer but does not explicitly bar intermediaries between issuers and holders from offering yield-like rewards funded by revenue-sharing arrangements with issuers.

The OCC proposal. The OCC's implementing proposal (91 Fed. Reg. 10199, March 2, 2026) restates the statutory prohibition and adds a rebuttable presumption with a two-prong trigger: prohibited yield is presumed where the issuer has an arrangement to pay interest or yield to an affiliate or a related third party, and that party in turn has an arrangement to pay interest or yield to a holder of the issuer's stablecoin. "Related third party" covers persons paying holders as a service on the issuer's behalf and white-label partners under whose branding the issuer issues. Two preamble sentences bound the rule's intended reach: the prohibition is not intended to prevent a merchant from independently offering a discount for paying in stablecoins, and it is, in the proposal's own words, "not intended to prevent a permitted payment stablecoin issuer from sharing in the profits derived from the payment stablecoin with a non-affiliate partner in a white-label arrangement." Arrangements outside the presumption can still be assessed case by case under the statute's anti-evasion authority.

The FDIC proposal. The FDIC's parallel proposal (91 Fed. Reg. 18534, April 10, 2026) prohibits the stablecoin issuers it supervises from paying interest or yield on stablecoins including via third-party arrangements. The formulation is broader on its face than the OCC's two-prong presumption. Which of the two rulebooks would govern OUSD is unknown, because the issuer of record, and therefore the supervisor of record, is undisclosed.

The pending legislation. The Senate-stage CLARITY Act print's Section 404 would extend a parallel prohibition to digital asset service providers and their affiliates, expressly excluding issuers: covered parties could not pay interest or yield, directly or indirectly, to US customers solely in connection with holding stablecoin balances or in a manner economically or functionally equivalent to deposit interest. Two carve-outs matter here: rewards based on bona fide activities or transactions that are not economically equivalent to deposit interest are permitted, and independent unaffiliated third parties are outside the prohibition unless a covered party directs or significantly influences the offering. A May 2026 joint letter from six bank trade associations argues this draft leaves structuring pathways open and asks Congress to delete the word "solely" and broaden the equivalence standard.

The two-hop map

Decompose the yield path into two hops. Hop one: issuer reserve revenue flows to a distribution partner. Hop two: the distribution partner pays something to an end holder. The four texts attach as follows:

Text What it reaches Does it reach the announced OUSD design?
GENIUS Act Section 4(a)(11) (enacted) Issuer-to-holder payments Not facially: the payee is a business being paid to distribute, not a holder being paid to hold
OCC proposed rule (March 2026) Both hops together: issuer pays a related third party, and that party pays holders No: the holder-facing second hop is not announced, and the preamble describes non-affiliate profit-sharing as outside the rule's intent
FDIC proposed rule (April 2026) Issuer paying yield on stablecoins, including via third-party arrangements Only if a business revenue-share is first characterized as yield on stablecoins at all
CLARITY Act Section 404 (pending) Hop two only: service-provider-to-customer payments No: it prohibits covered parties from paying customers, not from receiving issuer revenue share

The structural conclusion, stated as structure: the announced OUSD design occupies the one cell of the yield-routing matrix that the enacted statute, both pending implementing proposals, and the pending legislative amendment all leave unaddressed. Whether by design or not (this briefing takes no position on intent), the announced structure sits almost exactly on the far side of every line the current texts draw.

The two exposure points

The partner as holder. The strongest argument that hop one alone can violate the statute does not need any third-party extension: it recharacterizes the distributor as the holder. Lee Reiners (Duke) makes this argument for the Circle and Coinbase arrangement in a December 2025 analysis: under custody-law principles, federal guidance, and the exchange's own user agreement and litigation positions, the exchange rather than the customer is likely the holder of custodied tokens, and an issuer's balance-proportional revenue share to that exchange is, on his reading, in tension with the statute. The transferability to OUSD is direct: many OUSD launch partners would custody OUSD in omnibus wallets and would be on-chain holders of record. If the undisclosed calibration is proportional to partner-custodied balances, hop one is literally a payment to a holder measured by its holdings, and the dispute reduces to the statutory word "solely."

The word "solely," in both directions. OUSD's revenue share is conditioned on adopting and distributing the token, an ancillary activity beyond holding. On a narrow reading, that conditioning defeats "solely in connection with the holding, use, or retention" and takes even a balance-calibrated share outside the text. This is precisely the structuring pathway the bank coalition letter warned about when it asked Congress to delete "solely." The same feature cuts the other way before a regulator: a condition that consists of distributing the very token whose balances drive the payment may be read as use of the stablecoin, and the OCC proposal reserves case-by-case anti-evasion assessment for arrangements outside its presumption. Nothing in the announced design resolves either question.

The Coinbase contrast

The closest live analog is the Circle and Coinbase USDC arrangement. Under Circle's S-1 disclosures and contemporaneous reporting, Coinbase receives the reserve income on USDC held on its platform and half of residual reserve income elsewhere; Circle paid Coinbase $907.9 million in distribution fees in 2024, a majority of Circle's revenue that year; and Coinbase has marketed rewards for simply holding USDC on its platform. The contrast:

Feature Circle and Coinbase (USDC) Open Standard (OUSD, announced)
Hops Two: issuer to distributor, distributor to holder (customer rewards) One: issuer to distributors; no holder-facing payment announced
Parties Bilateral, negotiated, disclosed in the S-1 Multilateral, 140 or more partners, consortium-governed; terms undisclosed
Holder-facing yield Yes (advertised rewards rate) None announced
Calibration Balance-proportional (platform-held USDC) Undisclosed
OCC presumption (as proposed) Facially triggers both prongs; the issuer would bear the rebuttal burden Second prong unmet; the presumption does not attach
Partner-as-holder exposure Live (the Reiners argument targets it directly) Latent; activates if calibration is balance-proportional to custodied OUSD

Two implications. First, announced OUSD is one hop shorter than the arrangement the third-party debate has actually been about, so the incumbent arrangement is more exposed to the pending rule texts than the challenger's. Second, a final rule that squeezes hop two while leaving hop one open would push distribution economics from the rewards model toward the revenue-share model, a competitive inversion stated here in advance because it is checkable against post-rule behavior.

Four moves the final rules could make (due July 18, 2026)

  1. Finalize the OCC presumption as proposed. OUSD's announced design remains outside the presumption; the two-hop rewards structure remains presumptively prohibited subject to rebuttal. The boundary lands exactly where OUSD's design sits on the safe side, and distribution economics migrate from holder rewards to business revenue-share. The Senate amendment record supports this as the statute-faithful landing point.
  2. Broaden the trigger to balance-calibrated distributor compensation. Treat issuer payments to distributors measured by held or custodied balances as presumptive indirect yield, regardless of a holder-facing second hop; the FDIC's third-party-arrangements formulation is the natural vehicle. This reaches OUSD's hop one if and only if the undisclosed calibration is balance-proportional, and it would be litigated on the amendment record, since it extends by rule what the Senate declined to enact.
  3. Codify a holder definition. A rule defining holder by control (the custodial intermediary is the holder) arms the partner-as-holder theory against both USDC rewards and any balance-calibrated OUSD share. A rule defining holder by ultimate economic interest (the customer) defuses that theory and leaves hop one fully open. Neither proposal currently does this; the definitional silence is where the deepest uncertainty lives.
  4. Track the statute and refer the gap to Congress. Final rules restate the statutory prohibition, preserve case-by-case anti-evasion authority, and the third-party question rides on the CLARITY amendment. OUSD's design is undisturbed federally through the Act's effective date, and even an enacted Section 404 closes hop two, not hop one.

Under every move except a broad version of the second or a control-based version of the third, the announced design survives. That asymmetry, more than any single provision, is the finding: the design's viability is robust across most of the plausible rule space, and the two moves that would reach it are the two most exposed to statutory-authority challenge on the legislative record.

What this means for the yield debate

The Council of Economic Advisers' April 2026 white paper modeled the yield prohibition's stated rationale, protecting bank lending, and found the effect small: at baseline calibration, eliminating stablecoin yield increases bank lending by roughly $2.1 billion, about 0.02 percent of total loans. Combine that result with the OUSD design and a precise structural statement follows: the yield prohibition neither protects bank lending much, nor keeps reserve economics with issuers. Reserve revenue is flowing to whoever the boundary permits to compete for it, and under every design currently on the table, from issuer retention to distributor rewards to consortium revenue-share, holders are the one class excluded from that competition. That statement is checkable: if the final rules preserve hop one, reserve-yield capture should migrate toward distribution-layer intermediaries, observable in distribution-agreement disclosures and issuer cost structures.

What to watch

  1. Issuer of record disclosure (charter, license, supervisor). Determines which rulebook applies. Trigger: any application or state filing naming Open Standard or an affiliate.
  2. Revenue-share calibration basis (balances versus volume versus flat participation). Determines whether the partner-as-holder exposure is live. Trigger: consortium terms surfacing in partner disclosures; exchange quarterly filings are the likeliest surface.
  3. Final rule text on the presumption and any holder definition (July 18, 2026). The decision points above. Trigger: publication; score the four moves against it.
  4. Any partner announcing holder-facing OUSD rewards. Converts the design from one hop to two and attaches the presumption analysis. Trigger: partner marketing.
  5. CLARITY Section 404 enactment state. Closes hop two prospectively if enacted; hop one survives even that.

Limitations

  • This is an ex-ante briefing published before the final implementing rules, and it is written to be scored against them; a post-rule addendum is planned. It is a structural positioning analysis, not a formal academic paper.
  • OUSD is announced, not launched. The consortium agreements, the revenue-share calibration, and the issuer of record are undisclosed as of July 2, 2026; where a conclusion depends on an undisclosed parameter, the briefing says so.
  • This analysis characterizes where texts and design features sit relative to one another. It makes no allegation of misconduct, evasion, or bad faith against Open Standard, any consortium partner, Circle, Coinbase, or any other named party, and it is not legal advice to any entity. Several characterizations of legality are contested questions, labeled as contested.
  • Facts are as of July 2, 2026, drawn from the announcement and contemporaneous public reporting, the primary regulatory texts, Circle's S-1 disclosures as publicly reported, and the cited public legal commentary.
