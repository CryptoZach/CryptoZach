Minimum Viable Equivalence Pack: An Institutional Diligence Framework for Tokenized Products
The diligence floor for tokenized instruments used as collateral and cash

114-page PDF · ~60,000 words · SSRN

Description current as of June 2026 (SSRN v2).

Three tokenized U.S. Treasury products governed by three different regulatory regimes now receive comparable collateral treatment on the same institutional derivatives exchange, through the same custodian. A U.S. mutual fund under the Investment Company Act of 1940 (Franklin Templeton's BENJI), a BVI limited company (BlackRock's BUIDL), and a Cayman Islands fund explicitly unavailable to U.S. persons (Circle's USYC) all serve as eligible collateral in Binance's Portfolio Margin framework. The collateral framework does not distinguish between them, and no counterparty on the exchange, institutional or otherwise, receives disclosure of which instrument backs a position. The MVEP framework demonstrates that they are not equivalent.

At a glance

Question
When three tokenized Treasury products under three different regulatory regimes receive the same collateral treatment on the same exchange, can an institution treat them as equivalent?

Answer
No: the framework demonstrates the three products are not equivalent across legal, operational, and economic dimensions, and tested against a nine-product sample it finds that bank and 1940-Act-fund products satisfy the core requirements of nearly every category (BENJI fails only collateral operability, and the token-level Article 12 statutory defect reaches every product) while no stablecoin product does. The SVB weekend broke all three equivalence dimensions at once, with USDC trading at $0.87 and contagion cascading through at least five tokenized products. These are descriptive diligence findings: the framework is an entry gate that surfaces equivalence gaps before launch or committee approval, not a causal estimate.

Evidence base
The findings rest on ten diligence categories applied to a nine-product sample across three regulatory tiers, extended by five comparators that test the framework's reach (a consolidated fourteen-product scorecard), with the SVB episode reverse-engineered into the categories, the Binance Portfolio Margin collateral case (July 2025 to February 2026), a jurisdiction-differentiated Category 5 insolvency matrix (twenty regimes, twelve product-sample rows plus eight international comparators, scored across seven dimensions with each cell carrying a primary-source evidence basis), and independent corroboration from scholarly sources, the Federal Reserve's December 2025 FEDS Note, and Moody's first formal stablecoin rating methodology (March 2026).

Seven findings

1

The SVB weekend broke all three equivalence dimensions at once

Rights parity and claim priority broke legal equivalence; the system of record broke operational equivalence; and the depeg, with USDC trading at $0.87, broke economic equivalence. The cascade did not stop at USDC. The Federal Reserve’s December 2025 FEDS Note (Du, Sonawane, and Watsky, “In the Shadow of Bank Runs: Lessons from the Silicon Valley Bank Failure and Its Impact on Stablecoins,” FEDS Notes, December 17, 2025) documents that Dai’s Peg Stability Module, designed to stabilize Dai’s price, instead “created a direct channel of contagion from USDC to Dai.” The contagion then propagated through Dai’s additional PSMs to USDP and GUSD, stablecoins with zero direct SVB exposure. A single bank failure in Santa Clara cascaded through at least five tokenized products. The depeg resolved within 48 hours only because the FDIC invoked the systemic risk exception. Per the FEDS Note and Circle’s own disclosures, Circle’s cash reserves fell from approximately $11.5 billion to approximately $3.7 billion over three weeks despite the intervention; the backstop stopped the depeg but did not prevent a roughly $7.8 billion net outflow.

What this means: Equivalence can fail on every axis simultaneously and spread to products that never touched the failed bank, so an institution cannot treat a tokenized dollar as a safe substitute just because it held par yesterday.

2

Purpose-built federal frameworks satisfy nearly every diligence category; no stablecoin product does

The framework tests equivalence through ten diligence categories, each tied to a required artifact and an explicit pass or fail test. Applied to a nine-product sample and five comparators spanning three regulatory tiers, the framework produces seven findings. Products governed by purpose-built federal frameworks (banking law for the JPMorgan deposit token, the 1940 Act for BENJI) satisfy the core requirements of nearly every category. Bank depositors have FDIC insurance and statutory priority claims; stablecoin holders have contractual claims governed by terms of service that disclaim issuer liability across seventeen categories and characterize redemption as a revocable privilege. Stablecoin operators under the Money Transmission Modernization Act (MTMA), the model state statute drafted by the Conference of State Bank Supervisors and adopted in 31 states covering 99% of money transmission activity, operate on capital cushions roughly an order of magnitude thinner than banks under Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1). That regulatory-capital gap holds before accounting for the stress testing, deposit insurance, and resolution planning that banks face and money transmitters do not.

What this means: The regulatory regime a tokenized product sits under, not its marketing, determines whether it can clear diligence, so a risk committee should treat stablecoins and bank or fund products as categorically different rather than interchangeable collateral.

3

The Article 12 take-free defect is the one dimension where the stronger products do not pull ahead

On every other diagnostic dimension the bank deposit token and the 1940 Act fund outperform the stablecoin products; the take-free defect is the exception. Under UCC Article 12, a good-faith buyer of a tokenized instrument should acquire clean title even if a prior holder was a thief. But the statute requires the buyer to qualify through a “voluntary transaction creating an interest in property,” and a thief cannot create such an interest. The protection fails when it matters most. The defect was identified in 2022 and, consistent with the drafting history of UCC Article 12 as reflected in publicly available Uniform Law Commission meeting summaries, has been left unrepaired because the same defect would surface in UCC Article 8. No stablecoin issuer currently discloses it to holders.

What this means: A holder who buys a stolen tokenized instrument in good faith may not get the clean title the statute appears to promise, and because no issuer discloses this, buyers should not assume the take-free rule will protect them in a theft scenario.

4

MVEP is the entry gate and CLII is the drift detector

MVEP is the entry gate; the Control Layer Intensity Index (CLII) is the drift detector. A product can clear diligence at launch and still degrade through enforcement actions, reconciliation failures, custody issues, or governance drift. The framework pairs approval with trigger-based re-evaluation, with outcomes ranging from reaffirmation to conditional continuation to suspension of new issuance.

What this means: Clearing diligence once is not a permanent verdict, so an institution should pair an approval decision with ongoing monitoring that can downgrade or suspend a product when its control posture drifts.

5

“Offshore” was never a single insolvency category

The v2 paper replaces domicile-level insolvency characterization with a jurisdiction-differentiated Category 5 scoring matrix: twenty regimes, a twelve-row product sample (from the 1940 Act and the Federal Deposit Insurance Act through the BVI, Cayman, Bermuda, El Salvador, Singapore, and no-entity protocols, plus FDIC-insured-bank and OCC-trust-bank sub-rows) and eight international comparators (EU MiCA, the UK, Hong Kong, the UAE federal regime with its Abu Dhabi ADGM and Dubai VARA free zones, Switzerland, and Japan), scored across seven insolvency-posture dimensions (statutory segregation, claim priority, cross-border recognition, product-specific protections, rehypothecation constraints, forced-conversion risk, and recovery track record), each cell carrying a primary-source evidence basis. BENJI’s 1940 Act regime is the only one that passes all seven dimensions. USDT fails six of the seven: its issuer entities are domiciled in El Salvador, whose digital-asset regime contains no segregation, holder-priority, or rehypothecation provisions, while its terms are governed by BVI law and its reserves are custodied substantially in the United States, so a holder’s recovery path crosses three jurisdictions. And the largest tokenized Treasury product in the world, Circle’s USYC (approximately $3.0 billion held by only 47 holders), grades among the weakest on the recursive settlement and collateral-liquidation dimensions.

What this means: “Offshore” is not one risk bucket: two products with the same label can sit under regimes with materially different statutory protections, so insolvency diligence has to name the regime and score the dimensions, not the domicile.

6

Redemption pathway concentration may matter more than reserve composition under stress

Only 882 Tether accounts and 1,834 Circle accounts have direct redemption rights against their respective issuers; the remaining millions of holders must exit through secondary-market intermediaries whose aggregate liquidity is untested under stress. The March 2023 USDC depeg was driven not by inadequate reserves but by secondary-market holders who could not access direct redemption and therefore panic-sold on exchanges. The verified-account perimeter is persistent rather than a snapshot artifact: a monthly mint-and-redemption panel over the same window, and Ethena’s USDe operating a fixed allowlist of 20 authorized minter addresses while monthly mint volume scaled past $4 billion, show the redemption perimeter holding constant as scale grows.

What this means: Reserve attestations tell you what backs the token; they do not tell you who can actually redeem it, and in a stress event the second question is the one that determines whether the peg holds.

7

Federal charters relocate the claim, not the defect

The two federally chartered stablecoin structures the GENIUS Act anticipates, USD1 (issued through an OCC-chartered national trust bank) and SoFiUSD (the first stablecoin from an FDIC-insured national bank), each earn zero passing equivalence cells. The charter relocates the claim, to OCC receivership for the trust bank and to FDIC receivership for the insured bank, without curing the token-level rights-parity and settlement-finality defects that fail every fiat-backed stablecoin in the sample. SoFiUSD is expressly not a deposit, so deposit insurance and depositor preference never reach the holder; the insured charter relocates the claim-class question rather than resolving it.

What this means: A bank or trust charter changes where a holder stands in line if the issuer fails; it does not give a stablecoin holder the claim a depositor or fund shareholder has, so a charter is not a proxy for equivalence.

What this paper changes

Before an institution relies on a tokenized product, it should prove that the tokenized version maintains legal, operational, and economic equivalence, including under stress. Most tokenization diligence today is improvised: vendor decks standing in for diligence, one-off legal memos that are valuable but impossible to compare across proposals, and teams that build first and figure out custody, reconciliation, and exception handling later. MVEP replaces that with a versioned, comparable checklist centered on five committee questions:

  1. What is the authoritative record, and what happens when it disagrees with the chain?
  2. Who can freeze, rollback, or override, and what is the audit trail?
  3. What does "settled" mean here, both technically and economically?
  4. In insolvency, what exactly is the holder's claim, and in which jurisdiction?
  5. What are the dependencies that can break equivalence (redemption paths, transfer restrictions, pricing feeds), and what triggers failure?

If a proposal cannot answer all five with documented evidence, it goes back to the workstream. The emphasis across categories varies by the institution's role: an exchange evaluating collateral acceptance will focus on Category 10; a custodian will focus on Categories 4 and 5; a fund evaluating a composed product will require all ten with equal rigor.

A second change is definitional. The paper argues that settlement finality is broader than technical block confirmation. USDC kept settling on-chain during the SVB weekend, but the market value of those settlements diverged from par by 13%. Finality therefore has to include value integrity, convertibility conditions, halt triggers, reversal authority, and explicit loss allocation.

A third change is regulatory. The paper applies the framework to the OCC’s proposed implementing rule for the GENIUS Act (RIN 1557-AF41). The framework identifies what the proposed rule gets right (reserve composition, extension of federal supervision, Permitted Payment Stablecoin Issuer (PPSI) category differentiation), where it could be strengthened (holder-rights disclosure, interagency coordination on collateral eligibility, standardized equivalence assessments), and what remains unaddressed (the collateral eligibility boundary where instruments from different regulatory regimes receive comparable treatment without differentiation or disclosure). An OCC comment letter applying the framework to specific provisions of the proposed rule has been filed under Docket OCC-2025-0372 (Tokenization Systems comment, March 2026).

Where the framework does its heaviest work

The nine-product sample

The paper develops nine worked examples organized into three regulatory tiers with measurably different outcomes:

Tier 1 (purpose-built federal frameworks): The JPMorgan deposit token (JPMD), governed by federal banking law, and Franklin Templeton's BENJI (FOBXX), a registered mutual fund under the Investment Company Act of 1940. Both satisfy the core requirements of nearly every diagnostic category: the token-level Article 12 statutory defect reaches every product, and BENJI also fails collateral operability (no instant-redemption pool, business-hours-only redemption).

Tier 2 (regulated stablecoins): USDC (Circle), PYUSD (PayPal/Paxos), and USDG (Paxos/Global Dollar Network). Each passes some categories but fails Category 1 (Rights Parity) and Category 6 (Settlement Finality) on structural grounds independent of the regulatory regime.

Tier 3 (unregulated or decentralized): USDT (Tether) and DAI (MakerDAO). Both fail most categories. DAI inverts the pattern by passing the System of Record and monitoring categories (because its system of record is the blockchain itself) while failing the legal, redemption, and insolvency categories more acutely than any centralized product.

Additional worked examples: BUIDL (BlackRock/Securitize) and OUSG (Ondo Finance) are analyzed as composed products whose equivalence degrades to the weakest layer.

Tokenized Treasury bills and money market fund shares

This is where institutional volume is concentrating: BlackRock's BUIDL, Franklin Templeton's BENJI, Circle's USYC, and Ondo's OUSG and USDY. The primary use case is collateral mobility: posting a tokenized fund share as margin without liquidating the underlying position.

Three liquidation paths. Each has different availability, speed, and capacity. Path 1: an instant-redemption pool (Circle's smart contract exchanging BUIDL for USDC at par, 24/7, with a finite and fluctuating balance). Path 2: secondary market sale to another approved party via the transfer agent's alternative trading system. Path 3: standard fund redemption processed by the fund administrator against daily NAV, unavailable on weekends and holidays.

Four constraints determine which path is available and when. Pool capacity (the instant-redemption pool closes when depleted). Whitelist requirements (the instant-redemption and secondary market paths require the receiving wallet to be on the transfer agent's approved list). Cross-chain bridge risk (an exploit could create phantom balances that corrupt collateral valuations). Transfer restriction model (whitelist products exclude DeFi liquidation venues; blocklist products enable permissionless liquidation paths).

The collateral interoperability problem. Between approximately July 2025 and February 2026, Binance’s non-U.S. entities expanded the set of accepted off-exchange collateral to include three tokenized Treasury instruments: Circle’s USYC (a Cayman Islands fund, CIMA-licensed, issued by Circle International Bermuda Limited under BMA regulation, explicitly unavailable to U.S. persons under Regulation S), BlackRock’s BUIDL (a BVI limited company whose redemption routes primarily through Circle’s USDC facility), and Franklin Templeton’s BENJI (a 1940 Act mutual fund with statutory asset segregation). All three are yield-bearing, NAV-priced, and held through the same custodian (Ceffu). These are the acceptances Binance disclosed; the venue publishes no comprehensive or current account of its full eligible-collateral set, so whether it has grown since February 2026 is itself undisclosed, and the practice may be ongoing.

A counterparty to a collateralized trade cannot observe which instrument backs their position, receives no yield premium for the additional risk, and has no contractual right to specify which instrument is posted. Only the posting side of the arrangement is institutional: the other side of a futures trade can be any participant on the same order book, including non-institutional traders who receive no disclosure that off-exchange collateral is involved at all.

(Binance.US operates under different U.S.-specific policies; the broader market-direction signal is relevant to U.S. institutional diligence even where direct counterparty exposure is limited to offshore entities.)

No current or proposed federal regulation addresses this boundary. The OCC regulates PPSIs. The SEC regulates 1940 Act funds. Banking law regulates deposits. The CFTC regulates derivatives. Nobody supervises the seam where these instruments are combined in the same collateral pool.

Four distinct legal structures. Products in this asset class span at least four: a BVI-domiciled limited company under Reg D (BUIDL), a 1940 Act money market fund with statutory redemption rights (BENJI), a Delaware limited partnership with single-point-of-failure risk (OUSG), and a tokenized secured note where holders are senior secured creditors (USDY). Each structure requires a different evidence package for Rights Parity, Insolvency Posture, and Collateral Operability.

Seven stress scenarios test the constraints against specific liquidation triggers: margin call escalation, counterparty default, fund redemption cascade, regulatory seizure or sanctions, collateral substitution, cross-chain bridge incident, and operational NAV risk. Three scenario interaction chains show how scenarios compound.

The synthesis: the 2am Saturday margin call. The token can be posted as collateral instantly. But if it must be liquidated: the instant-redemption pool may be depleted, the secondary market may have no active market makers at 2am Saturday, and the fund administrator does not process redemptions until Monday. The MVEP must document the expected outcome when all three paths are degraded simultaneously and specify the loss allocation if conversion cannot complete before the margin deadline.

Tokenized private credit and receivables

Where the Treasury section demonstrates how operational and economic equivalence dominate, private credit introduces a different dominant failure mode: the token can be freely tradeable while the underlying claim requires procedural steps the token transfer does not trigger.

Earlier-stage but growing: Centrifuge, Maple, Goldfinch, Figure Technologies. Unlike T-bills, private credit has ongoing servicing obligations, covenant monitoring, and complex payment waterfalls. Under stress, the gap between what the token implies and what the holder can actually enforce becomes the central dispute.

Composed products: equivalence in layers

Each layer of a composed product requires its own MVEP assessment; the composed product's equivalence is the minimum across all layers. "We use BUIDL" falls short. "We use BUIDL, we have reviewed BlackRock's MVEP artifacts, and our product's equivalence posture degrades if any of the following conditions change" is the standard committees will expect.

The Category 5 jurisdiction matrix (new in v2)

The v2 revision replaces domicile-level insolvency characterization ("offshore") with a jurisdiction-differentiated scoring matrix: twenty regimes, the twelve product-sample regimes (the 1940 Act; the Federal Deposit Insurance Act; the U.S. trust-company and money-transmitter stack with the dormant GENIUS Act layer; Delaware LP / UCC Article 9; BVI; Cayman; Bermuda; El Salvador; Singapore; and no-entity protocols, plus the FDIC-insured-bank J2S and OCC-trust-bank J3F sub-rows) and eight international comparators (EU MiCA; the UK; Hong Kong; the UAE federal regime with its Abu Dhabi ADGM and Dubai VARA free zones; Switzerland; and Japan) scored across seven insolvency-posture dimensions, each cell carrying a verdict and a primary-source evidence basis verified against fetched primary texts. Product-level Category 5 verdicts derive from regime rows under a fixed aggregation rule, and fail-by-structural-inapplicability (a bank deposit presents no customer asset to segregate; a no-entity protocol presents no debtor, claim, or forum) is distinguished from fail-by-weak-regime so an inapplicability row reads as no regime rather than a weak one.

The full matrix ships as Exhibit 2, alongside the consolidated fourteen-product scorecard (Exhibit 1). Three of the scorecard's columns are GENIUS-era and charter comparators:

  • USD1 (column twelve): the largest dollar stablecoin classified under the GENIUS Act framework by platform taxonomy (approximately $4.36 billion, issued through BitGo Bank & Trust, N.A., an OCC-chartered uninsured national trust bank), a fully graded GENIUS-era comparator scored against the product's primary documents and on-chain contract state, sitting alongside the offshore USYC comparator.
  • SoFiUSD (column thirteen): the first stablecoin issued by an FDIC-insured full-service U.S. national bank (SoFi Bank, N.A.). The insured charter relocates rather than resolves the holder's claim-class question, because SoFiUSD is expressly not a deposit, so deposit insurance and depositor preference do not reach the holder, and Category 5 is scored on a dedicated insured-bank non-deposit sub-row (J2S) of the jurisdiction matrix.
  • RLUSD (column fourteen): the NYDFS-trust-chartered stablecoin from Ripple, a comparator carried as a candidate read, whose headline Non-Equivalent verdict holds on the same Category 1 rights-parity defect as the rest of the matrix.

The same revision also re-derives PYUSD onto the federal-layer-only trust-bank stack: its issuer's OCC conversion is now consummated, so the New York custody and rehypothecation anchors no longer reach it and its custody and insolvency verdicts move off the state-supervised basis.

The composite rating (Exhibit 3)

The scorecard aggregates into a per-product rating, presented as Exhibit 3. It is deliberately not a weighted score. Averaging the categories would be compensatory: a strong custody score could buy back a failed rights-parity cell, which is exactly the inference the framework rejects, because the categories measure independent failure modes. The rating is therefore non-compensatory. Each of the three equivalence dimensions (legal, operational, economic) is graded on its weakest cell, so a fatal failure caps the dimension and cannot be offset elsewhere. A separate strength score from 1.0 to 5.0 (the average cell quality within the dimension) orders products inside a tier without ever lifting the tier, the way a credit rating's notch works under its letter grade. The headline is the weakest of the three.

Every product rates Non-Equivalent at the headline, and that is the finding, not a flaw in the rating: the market-wide UCC Article 12 rights-parity defect fails Category 1 for every product except JPMD, so no tokenized dollar product clears legal equivalence. The discrimination lives in the strength score and in the operational and economic profiles. Read that way the rating reproduces the three regulatory tiers the paper identifies: the tokenized funds are strongest, the regulated stablecoins sit in the middle, and USDT is weakest. Two products show instructive splits. DAI is operationally strong but legally floored: its protocol state is an authoritative record, yet it has no legal entity to hold a claim against. JPMD is the only product whose legal and economic dimensions escape Non-Equivalent, both landing at Indeterminate, which makes the bank deposit token the closest to passing; it is held back at the headline only by an on-chain governance failure.

Governance and monitoring

Governance and change control

Single-signer admin keys are disqualifying. The proposed institutional standard (not currently met by any observed market leader): admin operations affecting token economics or holder rights require 3-of-5 multisig; core logic changes require 4-of-7 or equivalent. Signers must be identified individuals with named roles.

Proposed timelock minimums: 48 hours for parameter changes, 7 days for logic upgrades, 30 days for migrations. Emergency overrides require a higher signer threshold, independent audit within 72 hours, and immediate holder notification.

These thresholds represent a proposed institutional standard, not observed market practice. On-chain verification of the four largest tokenized Treasury products reveals no timelocks in any admin chain. Operators approximating partial compliance (multisig but no timelocks; or timelocks on logic but not parameters) include selected Tier 1 and Tier 2 issuers.

Continuous monitoring: CLII as the drift detector

Where MVEP answers "is equivalence proven now?", the CLII answers "is the control layer degrading over time?" The CLII scores each gateway on five weighted control dimensions (regulatory licensing, reserve transparency, freeze and blacklist capability, compliance infrastructure, and geographic restrictions); when a gateway's score drops below a defined threshold, the product is flagged for review, and counterparties must be notified within 24 hours of initiating that review.

The MSB capital framework

State money services business law was designed to regulate wire transfer operators: firms that hold customer funds transiently while a transfer clears. Stablecoin issuers hold customer funds permanently. Under the MTMA (adopted by 31 states covering 99% of money transmission activity), a money transmitter holding $40 billion in customer funds faces a minimum tangible net worth requirement of approximately $216 million (0.54% of total assets). A bank holding the same $40 billion would be required to maintain approximately $4.8 billion in CET1 capital (12%). The gap is approximately 22 to 1, before accounting for the stress testing, deposit insurance, and resolution planning requirements that banks face and money transmitters do not.

When Circle disclosed $3.3 billion in exposure to Silicon Valley Bank, that single exposure exceeded the issuer's total stockholders' equity. The Federal Reserve's own analysis notes that Circle faced "the possibility of a significant shortfall" without a public guarantee on uninsured deposits. The capital framework would not have absorbed the loss.

Independent corroboration

The framework's findings are independently corroborated by five lines of external work:

  • Odinet and Tosato identify four critical private law deficiencies in centralized stablecoin governance: asymmetrical terms of service, ambiguous customer rights, tenuous redemption systems, and perilous standing in issuer bankruptcy. Separately, the foundational private-law treatment by Bruce, Odinet, and Tosato builds an original dataset of the leading issuers' terms of service and shows that holders' contractual and reserve-asset claims are typically weaker than front-facing materials represent. These four deficiencies correspond to MVEP Categories 8, 1, 4, and 5.
  • The Moneyness paper's five targeted reforms map one-to-one onto five MVEP categories. Five reforms, five categories, developed independently by different researchers using different methodologies, arriving at the same five structural deficiencies.
  • The Federal Reserve's December 2025 FEDS Note on SVB and stablecoins independently documents the PSM contagion channel, the redemption pathway concentration problem, and the two-way contagion risk between stablecoins and Treasury markets.
  • Moody's first formal stablecoin rating methodology (Klotz and Hu, March 2026) converges on the framework from an independent starting point: its effective-segregation rating-eligibility threshold tracks Category 5, its operational-risk gate and advance-rate appendix track Categories 4 and 10, and its liquidation-trigger haircut differential quantifies the value of the collateral-operability architecture the framework tests.
  • The macro-financial channel is now empirically documented: Ahmed and Aldasoro (BIS, 2025) measure state-dependent Treasury-yield effects of stablecoin flows that are largest exactly when forced liquidation would be most damaging; the Council of Economic Advisers (2026) quantifies the holder-rights side of the channel; and the American Bankers Association's rebuttal of the CEA framing (the live counterfactual is yield allowed at scale, not yield prohibited at today's scale) shows the policy debate turning on the same routing-and-rights questions the framework operationalizes.

How the paper is built

  • Three equivalence claims (legal, operational, economic) tested independently
  • SVB episode reverse-engineered into categories one through nine; tenth derived from subsequent collateral mobility use case
  • Nine-product worked examples across three regulatory tiers
  • Cross-product comparison: four legal structures, three yield mechanisms, whitelist vs. blocklist transfer restriction models
  • Composed-product analysis: Categories 2, 4, and 10 traced through all layers
  • Prescriptive governance thresholds benchmarked against on-chain verification of current market leaders
  • Collateral interoperability analysis anchored on verified Binance evidence (USYC, BUIDL, BENJI)
  • Jurisdiction-differentiated Category 5 scoring matrix: twenty regimes by seven dimensions, every cell primary-source based (Exhibit 2)
  • Consolidated fourteen-product, eleven-row verdict scorecard (Exhibit 1), including the offshore USYC, GENIUS-era USD1, insured-national-bank SoFiUSD, and NYDFS-trust-chartered RLUSD comparators
  • Independent corroboration from five sources (Odinet and Tosato on private-law deficiencies; the Moneyness paper's five reforms; the Fed FEDS Note; Moody's rating methodology; and the macro-financial channel in Ahmed and Aldasoro, the Council of Economic Advisers, and the American Bankers Association)
  • Applied test: OCC comment letter on GENIUS Act implementing rule (RIN 1557-AF41)
  • Framework paper anchored on a failure case; the findings describe observed patterns and should be read as evidence consistent with the framework, not as proof of causal relationships

Each row is marked "pass," "in progress" (with target date), or "not applicable" (with written justification). Any "in progress" item means the proposal goes back to the workstream.

3 Record-keeping and discrepancy resolution. How often are records compared, and what happens when they disagree? Reconciliation schedule, discrepancy resolution procedures, and discrepancy categories ranked by severity. Must distinguish timing discrepancies (records temporarily disagree and will converge) from integrity discrepancies (records disagree because one is wrong and will not self-correct).
For tokens on multiple blockchains: per-chain reconciliation and cross-chain balance checks. For yield-distributing tokens: procedures for supply changes caused by dividend mints. The reconciliation surface scales with the number of deployment chains; products in this asset class range from 4 to over 12 chains.		 Timing discrepancies close before any collateral valuation or liquidation event. Integrity discrepancies trigger defined halt procedures (stop extending credit, freeze affected balances, escalate). No unresolved unknowns across any deployment chain.
4 Asset segregation and custody. Are client assets separated from the custodian's own assets, and who controls movement? Segregation model, movement controls, and custody agreement. Customer property treatment is explicit, has been verified under a documented stress scenario (custodian failure or enforcement action), and is consistent with disclosures.
5 Claim priority in insolvency. If the issuer, custodian, or intermediary fails, what is the tokenholder's claim and where does it rank? Bankruptcy/receivership treatment memo and scenario analysis covering each intermediary in the custody chain. Claim priority is clear at each intermediary. Rehypothecation risk (the custodian reusing client assets) and forced conversion risk (the token being converted to cash or another form in restructuring) are explicit.
6 Settlement finality. What does "settled" mean for this token, and does the definition hold when the pricing feed is disrupted? Definition of "settled" covering value integrity, convertibility, halt triggers, reversal authority, and loss allocation. Operational runbook. Pricing data dependency map naming providers, maximum acceptable staleness, fallback method, and defined behavior when the primary source is unavailable or disputed. Finality criteria and reversal authority are explicit. Value integrity is verifiable even when the pricing feed is disrupted.
7 Rollback and exception response. If the blockchain retroactively replaces confirmed transactions (a reorg), what happens? Reorg/rollback response plan (required if the token uses public blockchains) specifying: detection mechanism, halt criteria, loss allocation between parties, communication timelines, and the process for reconciling the share register with the post-reorg chain state. Halt triggers, loss allocation, and communication timelines are pre-agreed. The plan has been tested or tabletop-exercised.
8 Governance and change control. Who can change the smart contract's rules, and is there a record that the process was followed? Change control policy, admin key policy (identifying key holders by name and role), and audit logs of every admin operation since launch. Admin operations require 3-of-5 multisig (multiple required signers) approval; core logic changes require 4-of-7. Timelocks enforced: 48 hours (parameter changes), 7 days (logic upgrades), 30 days (migrations). Every change is logged, approved through the documented process, and reversible. The audit log demonstrates continuous compliance since launch.
9 Stress-scenario disclosures. Do the disclosures describe what happens under stress, or only during normal operations? Token-specific disclosure addendum matching actual product behavior under stress scenarios (reserve concentration, convertibility disruption, pricing delays, custody failure). Disclosures match how the product actually works under stress. No material risk identified in Categories 1 through 8 and 10 is absent from the disclosure.
10 Collateral liquidation readiness. If the collateral must be converted to cash under stress, can it be liquidated within the contractual deadline? Do the scenarios compound? Collateral agreement review covering: custody model (triparty vs. bilateral); the three liquidation paths (instant-redemption pool, secondary market sale, standard fund redemption) with capacity limits and availability windows; four constraints on path availability (pool capacity, whitelist requirements, cross-chain bridge risk, and transfer restriction model including whether the product uses a whitelist or blocklist architecture and whether the restriction contract is upgradeable); pre-authorized path priority and execution time per scenario; blocked-property provisions and loss allocation when collateral is frozen by a regulator; netting enforceability opinion covering sanctions scenarios; escalation ladder from haircut increase through forced liquidation; pool and market-maker capacity relative to large counterparty positions; dividend distribution handling; yield-mechanism documentation (mint-based, accumulating, or rebasing) with oracle dependency and stale-price risk during weekends or holidays; and scenario interaction chains documenting how scenarios compound.
For composed products: trace the redemption chain, custody chain, and registrar of record through every layer; document whether the composed product's rate limits exceed the underlying pool's capacity. For products with business-hours-only redemption: pre-agreed loss allocation for the hours gap and interim collateral credit arrangements. The collateral agreement must be reviewed against this category before committee; silence on any point means "in progress."		 At least one liquidation path can execute under each documented stress scenario within the contractual deadline. Scenario interaction chains have defined escalation procedures. When no path can complete in time, the loss allocation is explicit and pre-agreed in the collateral agreement.

How to read this table

Timing vs. integrity discrepancies (Category 3). Six mechanisms can cause the share register, on-chain balances, and cross-chain balances to disagree. Four are timing discrepancies that will self-correct: an on-chain transaction confirming before the transfer agent's systems record it; a redemption approved by the transfer agent whose on-chain burn has not yet confirmed; a cross-chain transfer in transit between burn on one chain and mint on another; and a dividend distribution propagating across chains at different confirmation speeds. Two are integrity discrepancies that will not self-correct: a bridge exploit creating phantom token balances on one chain; and a chain reorganization reversing a transfer the register still shows as complete. The required reconciliation cadence and response procedures differ by type.

Scenario interaction chains (Category 10). Three documented chains: a bridge incident triggers industry-wide collateral substitution, which triggers a fund redemption cascade as multiple parties redeem through the same pool simultaneously; a sanctions designation triggers counterparty default, which triggers margin calls on correlated positions at the same exchange; an operational NAV disruption triggers collateral substitution, which triggers a fund redemption cascade if the substitution is industry-wide.

Four constraints on path availability (Category 10). Pool capacity (the instant-redemption pool closes when depleted); whitelist requirements (the instant-redemption and secondary market paths require the receiving wallet to be approved); cross-chain bridge risk (a bridge exploit could corrupt collateral valuations); and transfer restriction model (whitelist products exclude DeFi liquidation venues, blocklist products enable permissionless paths, and the restriction contract may be upgradeable after collateral is posted).

Composed products (Categories 2, 4, 10). Each layer requires its own MVEP assessment. Category 2 must identify the registrar of record at every layer (the wrapper may lack a named transfer agent). Category 4 must trace the custody chain through all layers. Category 10 must trace the redemption chain (the composed product's instant redemption may depend on the underlying product's pool, and the composed product's rate limits may exceed the pool's capacity).

Business-hours-only products (Category 10). Products with no instant-redemption pool and no secondary market have no available liquidation path outside business hours. The pass condition requires pre-agreed loss allocation for the hours gap.

Limitations

The framework is intentionally minimal and, in the paper's phrasing, "designed to be wrong in known ways." Seven predictable failure modes:

  • Checkbox compliance: Artifacts exist on paper but do not reflect reality. The framework requires demonstrated use.
  • Stale equivalence: Products pass at launch but drift. Mandatory periodic re-certification addresses this.
  • Category blindness: Ten categories are comprehensive for current structures, but novel products may introduce risks that do not fit. A "residual risk" disclosure covers this gap.
  • False precision: Pass/fail creates an illusion of certainty when equivalence is a spectrum. An MVEP pass means the minimum standard is met.
  • Jurisdictional scope: Three categories (Rights Parity, Insolvency Posture, System of Record) require jurisdiction-specific analysis for cross-border distribution.
  • Disclosure regime dependence: An evaluator's ability to verify Category 9 independently depends on the governing regime. Products under the Investment Company Act file public reports; products under Reg D or Reg S (exemptions for private placements and offshore offerings) disclose only to onboarded investors.
  • Structural scope: The framework is calibrated for tokenized fund shares but also applies to tokenized secured notes, where the insolvency posture is fundamentally different. A secured noteholder is a senior secured creditor, with different Category 5 evidence requirements.

The framework's value is in making the standard explicit, the gaps visible, and the failure modes predictable.
