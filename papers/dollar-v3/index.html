Dollar v3: How Stablecoin Policy Is About to Re-Write the U.S. Dollar

7 min read·December 2025·Zach Zukowski

Medium · Dec 2025 · Policy / Payments / Stablecoins

Description current as of March 2026.

Payment stablecoins, tokenized deposits, and yield wrappers may look interchangeable in a wallet, but diverge in reserve structure, redemption mechanics, legal treatment, and crisis behavior.

This page explains why policy attaches to the control layer, not to the token label, and which regulated functions GENIUS and Interpretive Letter 1188 actually reach.

1

Architecture redesign, not a new currency, is the policy event. Dollar v1 was cash: bearer instrument, physical, hard to surveil, hard to reverse. Dollar v2 was bank deposits plus card networks: the bank account became the unit of everyday money, the payment network became the routing layer, intermediaries controlled access and data. Dollar v3 is internet-native dollars on always-on programmable rails: stablecoins, deposit tokens, and tokenized cash-management wrappers routed through wallets, APIs, and compliance systems. Each architectural generation distributes power, captures spread, and enables surveillance differently. The policy question for v3 is not "does the dollar win" (it can win in any of these architectures) but "who wins inside v3, and who quietly subsidizes the rest." That question is decided by container design, not currency choice.

2

Layer 0 is the hidden variable. Two digital-dollar products can look identical in a consumer wallet and behave very differently in a panic because reserves sit in different places, mobilize on different timetables, and carry different backstop expectations. The essay's term, deposit beta, captures how much of a stablecoin's reserve flows back into the banking system as deposits versus sits in non-bank assets. The same one-dollar token has different macroeconomic effects depending on where its reserve dollar lives.

3

The likely end state is hybrid, not monoculture. Scenario for 2030 to 2035: payment stablecoins serve as the 24/7 cash leg, yield migrates to clearly labeled wrappers, bank deposit tokens upgrade programmable bank money inside the existing perimeter, and offshore dollar coins persist at the edges. This is the architecture the GENIUS Act perimeter and the OCC Interpretive Letter 1188 framework appear designed to produce. The policy question is which container captures the consumer interface, which captures the institutional clearing layer, and which captures the offshore corridor.

At a glance

Question
Payment stablecoins, tokenized deposits, and yield wrappers may look interchangeable in a wallet. Are they?

Answer
No. They diverge in reserve structure, redemption mechanics, legal treatment, and crisis behavior. The essay locates Dollar v3 (internet-native dollars on always-on programmable rails) as the third architectural generation after v1 (cash) and v2 (bank deposits and card networks); architecture redesign, not a new currency, is the policy event. Policy attaches to the control layer (reserve custody, settlement access, routing defaults, screening, holds, disclosures, recordkeeping), not to the token label. A bank deposit token, a 1940 Act fund wrapper, and a payment stablecoin trigger different regulatory regimes regardless of how they appear in the same UI.

Evidence base
Policy essay anchored on three sources: the Federal Reserve's December 2025 FEDS Note (Du, Sonawane, Watsky, "In the Shadow of Bank Runs," DOI 10.17016/2380-7172.3958), the enacted GENIUS Act payment-stablecoin perimeter, and OCC Interpretive Letter 1188 on bank activity on crypto rails. Not an empirical study with a dataset.

Implication
Policy designers and operator counsel should map which control-layer functions trigger which regulatory regime before token-label classification debates. The token may stay the same; the regime changes when reserve composition, routing default, or settlement access changes. Regulatory perimeter follows the operator's compliance posture, not the token's denomination.

What this piece changes

Most stablecoin commentary asks whether stablecoins are good or bad, or whether banks or crypto firms will “win.” This piece changes the unit of analysis. It treats the emerging system as market-structure design: who can issue, where reserves can sit, where yield is allowed to live, who owns the last-mile wallet relationship, who gets access to settlement rails, and who markets expect to be rescued when something breaks.

It also broadens the use case beyond retail payments. One of the strongest sections argues that stablecoins are increasingly a settlement primitive: the always-on cash leg for tokenized Treasuries, money-market funds, credit, and eventually broader securities markets.

Core arguments

1. Three dollar objects, three rulebooks

The distinction is legal and operational. Payment stablecoins are framed as par-redeemable payment infrastructure. Deposit tokens remain inside the banking perimeter. Yield wrappers belong under securities- and funds-style governance. The GENIUS Act (Public Law 119-27, enacted July 18, 2025) reinforces the payment-stablecoin side of that separation by requiring liquid reserve backing (per the Act’s reserve composition section), monthly public reserve disclosures, 1:1 redemption, restrictions on misleading marketing, and priority for stablecoin holders in insolvency (per the Act’s Bankruptcy Code amendments). Primary rulemaking is in progress across OCC, FDIC, FinCEN, and Treasury as of April 2026. Deposit tokens and yield wrappers still require distinct banking- and securities-style treatment.

2. Deposit effects are about funding structure, not outflows alone

The essay’s “deposit mutation” argument is the author’s synthesis of the Federal Reserve’s December 2025 argument that stablecoins can reduce, recycle, or restructure deposits, including by shifting bank funding toward more concentrated, uninsured, wholesale balances. Even if aggregate deposits hold steady, funding composition can become more runnable as stablecoin-serving banks absorb reserve deposits. That is the mechanism through which stablecoin growth can tighten credit conditions even without a dramatic headline run on deposits. Each issuer holds reserves at different banks, and the monetary impact depends on which issuer absorbs the growth.

3. Layer 0 determines panic behavior

The essay repeatedly returns to the idea that reserve location, Fed access, and backstop expectations determine who absorbs stress. Two products can share the same wallet UX and diverge under stress because the real variable is underneath: whether reserves sit in bank deposits, T-bills, repo, or more Fed-adjacent cash; whether liquidity can move intraday; and whether markets expect a backstop. This aligns with the broader control-layer research program: the same top-line product behaves differently depending on where reserves sit and how redemption works operationally.

4. Five policy levers determine the future, not one “stablecoin bill”

The essay’s organizing framework: who can issue in practice, where yield can live, whether the system defaults to a private stack or a public spine, how offshore dollar coins are handled, and whether rails stay open or become walled gardens. These five levers interact: tightening one shifts pressure to others.

5. Bank intermediation is moving closer to the rails

OCC Interpretive Letter 1188 (November 2023) states that national banks may engage in riskless principal crypto-asset transactions, but only in a safe and sound manner and in compliance with applicable law. (Riskless principal is a legal classification under banking law for transactions in which a bank takes a position that is offset by a contemporaneous transaction with a third party. It does not mean the bank bears no risk; market risk is neutralized but operational, custody, and counterparty risks remain.) That supports the essay’s bank-centric path: banks can become execution, custody, and compliant-distribution layers for the tokenized economy even without a retail CBDC. The essay is careful that “riskless principal” is a legal classification, not proof that operations remain low-risk under correlated outages.

Value capture: float versus home screen

The essay’s sharpest commercial argument: if issuance becomes standardized and competitive, the distribution layer (wallets, apps, defaults, routing, sweeps, and compliance UX) captures the economics. If Layer 0 privilege and backstop expectations concentrate issuance, issuers capture more of the rent and too-connected-to-fail dynamics emerge. The organizing fight is issuer spread on reserves versus the app layer that controls defaults, routing, sweeps, and user-visible economics.

Rights framework and scoreboard

This is partly a normative piece. It proposes a rights minimum viable specification for a citizen-friendly Dollar v3: due-process freezes, data minimization, a small-value privacy zone, real portability and interoperability, and transparency obligations for systemically important issuers and wallets.

The scoreboard defines practical evaluation criteria: par redemption, clear claim priority, reserve transparency, stress-tested liquidity, portability, interoperability, and macro-stability monitoring. These are core outputs of the essay, not appendices.

Anchor evidence

This is a policy essay, not an empirical paper with a dataset. It is anchored on:

  • Federal Reserve December 2025 FEDS Note (Du, Sonawane, and Watsky, “In the Shadow of Bank Runs: Lessons from the Silicon Valley Bank Failure and Its Impact on Stablecoins,” FEDS Notes, December 17, 2025, DOI 10.17016/2380-7172.3958) on stablecoin deposit effects, which frames outcomes as deposits being reduced, recycled, or restructured depending on demand source and reserve management.
  • GENIUS Act (enacted), which sets the federal payment-stablecoin perimeter around liquid reserve backing, monthly public disclosures, 1:1 redemption, marketing limits, and holder priority in insolvency.
  • OCC Interpretive Letter 1188 (December 2025), which states that national banks may engage in riskless principal crypto-asset transactions, subject to safe-and-sound and legal-compliance requirements.
  • CLARITY Act (H.R. 5403, 119th Congress), analyzed in the companion piece through its House-passed form and official section-by-section summaries as a market-structure overlay, separate from a dedicated payment-stablecoin title. As of April 2026: passed the House; Senate consideration is ongoing. Readers should check Congress.gov for current status.

Scenario numbers (2030 to 2035 hybrid stack, deposit-shift estimates, adoption paths) are the author’s strategic judgment within these frameworks; they are not measured outcomes.

Limitations

  • This is a policy essay expressing the author’s analytical framework and strategic judgment; it is not an empirical study with testable hypotheses.
  • “Deposit mutation” is the author’s term; the Federal Reserve describes the underlying mechanism as deposits being reduced, recycled, or restructured.
  • The 2030 to 2035 hybrid end state and any multi-trillion-scale projections are scenario architecture, conditional on legislative and regulatory outcomes that remain uncertain.
  • The five-lever framework is an organizing heuristic; policy levers interact in ways the essay acknowledges but cannot fully model.
  • The GENIUS Act governs the payment-stablecoin perimeter; deposit tokens and yield wrappers require separate banking and securities-style treatment that remains in development.
