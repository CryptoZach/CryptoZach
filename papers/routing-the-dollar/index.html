Routing the Dollar
Gateway Infrastructure, Monetary Policy Transmission, and the Dollar’s International Functions

March 2026 · SSRN · Prepared for the Fifth Conference on International Roles of the U.S. Dollar,
Federal Reserve Board and Federal Reserve Bank of New York, June 2026

Description current as of March 2026.

The same dollar token can carry different regulatory obligations, different Fed-policy exposure, and different crisis behavior depending on which issuer, exchange, or protocol routes it.

This page explains why the gateway, not the token, is the policy-relevant unit.

At a glance

Question
When digital dollars scale, what matters more: the token itself, or the gateway that routes it?

Answer
The gateway matters more. The token may stay the same, but the routing layer changes the regulatory surface, the Fed-policy channel, and the way stress propagates. These are descriptive findings, not causal estimates.

Evidence base
Daily Ethereum transfer data tracking 19 gateway entities across 51 wallet addresses (covering approximately 27.7% of total USDC and USDT on-chain transfer volume on Ethereum mainnet over the 157-week sample window, per Artemis-filtered data; specific Dune query reproducibility pending verification, see DATA_REGISTRY), monthly maps of which gateways transact with each other (15 gateways, 35 months, 3,804 unique pairs), stablecoin transfer volume across nine blockchains ($60.6T cumulative from February 2023 through January 2026, filtered by Artemis Analytics to remove spam and bot activity), and two stress episodes (SVB crisis and BUSD regulatory action).

The problem

Dollar stablecoins now move across blockchains, exchanges, protocols, and regulatory jurisdictions at meaningful scale. Most debates still treat each stablecoin as a product to classify.

This paper argues that the more useful unit is the gateway: the issuer, exchange, or protocol that actually routes the token. Once you look at that layer, the same dollar token can behave very differently under tightening cycles, supervisory pressure, and crisis conditions.

Three findings

1

Stablecoin supply moves through a dollar-specific, regime-dependent policy channel

Stablecoin supply and Federal Reserve balance-sheet variables share a long-run relationship (cointegration) that is regime-dependent: strongest during quantitative tightening (when the Fed is shrinking its balance sheet) and weaker during easing (when rates are falling). The same relationship does not appear for Bitcoin or Ethereum, which points to a dollar-specific channel. A yield-spread mechanism helps explain the result: the gap between Treasury bill yields and on-chain lending rates forecasts stablecoin supply growth, consistent with issuers expanding supply when the reserve spread widens. No Federal Reserve policy announcement effect survives correction for multiple testing, so the evidence sits in the slower equilibrium and yield-spread channels.

What this means: Stablecoins already sit inside a dollar transmission channel, but the channel runs through issuer reserve economics and gateway structure, not headline announcements alone.

2

Gateway choice changes the regulatory and concentration outcome

The Control Layer Intensity Index classifies regulatory intensity across 19 gateway entities on licensing, reserve transparency, freeze capability, compliance infrastructure, and geographic restrictions. Over three years, transfer volume doubled while the number of distinct counterparties fell by about one quarter and cross-gateway connections dropped from 5.8% to 2.6%. One market-making firm’s share of identified counterparty volume rose from 1.4% to 19.9%. Tier 1 is effectively a Coinbase-Circle duopoly (56%/44% of Tier 1 volume).

Usage expanded. Control consolidated.

What this means: Aggregate stablecoin supply is too blunt to monitor the system well. Gateway-level flow and concentration data reveal the control layer that aggregate numbers do not capture.

3

Stress episodes caused rotation across dollar gateways, not exit from dollar-pegged instruments

In the monitored Ethereum gateway sample, the share of volume routed through the most heavily regulated gateways fell from 63% to 13% during the SVB crisis and then recovered to 57% after banking-sector interventions. Circle and Coinbase had near-identical CLII scores but showed sharply different stress retention, consistent with entity-specific banking exposure mattering more than compliance tier. Three gateways with zero SVB exposure (MakerDAO, Paxos, Gemini) still saw their stablecoins depeg through reserve-chain contagion.

During the BUSD wind-down (the cleaner test, because Paxos guaranteed 1:1 redemption throughout and there was no peg pressure), users migrated toward USDT and Binance-adjacent routing, not toward higher-CLII alternatives, suggesting exchange continuity can matter more than regulatory intensity. Wintermute’s role in stablecoin secondary-market routing expanded materially during the wind-down, as routing volume previously flowing through BUSD migrated across alternative stablecoin pairs. The Wintermute main wallet (0xDBF5E9c5206d0dB70a90108bf936DA60221dC080, Blockscout-verified) concentrated a growing share of post-BUSD volume; precise volume-migration percentages require a dedicated methodology not reconstructed in this paper, and interested readers can audit the wallet directly.

What this means: The CLII classifies where regulatory obligations attach, but compliance posture and stress fragility are separate questions. Assessing both requires measuring at the gateway level.

Who should care

For policymakers: Monitor gateway flows, banking dependencies, and settlement chokepoints, not aggregate stablecoin supply alone. The control layer is where obligations attach and where correlated exposure becomes visible. Three types of automatic rules deserve particular attention: default sweep destinations, routing preferences, and hold triggers.

For issuers and operators: Reserve composition and bank placement matter, but so do routing defaults and continuity under stress. The monetary impact of stablecoin growth depends on which issuer absorbs it. Deposit recycling (a derived metric, the share of stablecoin reserves held as bank deposits) varies sharply by issuer: approximately Tether 4%, Circle 14%, Gemini 100%. Methodology documentation pending; deposit recycling is not directly reported in issuer attestations and is computed from on-chain flow analysis combined with attestation disclosures (see DATA_REGISTRY).

For investors and market-structure teams: The CLII classifies compliance posture; stress resilience requires separate assessment. A gateway can score highly on compliance and still prove fragile if its reserve chain, banking counterparties, or routing dependencies are weak.

For payments and growth teams: Per Artemis classification labels (specific time window pending verification, see DATA_REGISTRY): identified merchant payments are approximately 0% of adjusted on-chain volume as a lower bound, peer-to-peer transfers account for approximately 46%, and decentralized finance activity approximately 33%. Point estimates are directional; classification methods label part of volume, with the remainder treated as unassigned. The market is still dominated by transfer, liquidity, and settlement uses.

Who routes the dollar matters more than which dollar is routed.

How the paper is built

  • 157-week panel across nine blockchains (February 2023 to January 2026)
  • 51-address Ethereum counterparty registry (19 entities, capturing 27.7% of USDC/USDT volume)
  • Monthly counterparty networks (15 gateways, 3,804 unique transaction-pair relationships)
  • Johansen cointegration on a three-variable system (Fed total assets, overnight reverse repo balances, stablecoin supply), testing whether these variables share a stable long-run equilibrium. (Cointegration: a long-run equilibrium relationship between non-stationary time series. Johansen trace: a statistical test for the number of cointegrating relationships among variables.)
  • Application-level flow decomposition: Ethereum, Solana, and Base (64% of volume)
  • Five selection-bias robustness checks including Nansen cross-validation (rank correlation = 0.94, meaning the two independent data sources nearly perfectly agree on ordering)
  • Two stress-episode windows: SVB crisis and BUSD regulatory action
  • 19 FOMC meetings tested for announcement effects; none survives correction for multiple testing

Full technical summary

What this paper changes

Most stablecoin analysis focuses on the token: classification, reserve composition, market capitalization. This paper shifts the unit of analysis to the gateway: the issuer, exchange, or protocol that actually routes stablecoin value.

Once you measure at that layer, the same dollar token produces different regulatory exposure, different monetary-policy transmission, and different stress behavior.

The Control Layer Intensity Index (CLII) supports that shift by classifying regulatory intensity across licensing, reserve transparency, freeze capability, compliance infrastructure, and geographic restrictions. The CLII is a classification heuristic for compliance posture; it is not a resilience score and not a predictive model of stress outcomes.

These are descriptive findings. None should be read as causal estimates.

1. Monetary-policy transmission is real, dollar-specific, and regime-dependent

Stablecoin supply and Federal Reserve balance-sheet variables move together in a long-run equilibrium relationship (cointegration) that is dollar-specific and regime-dependent. The paper measures this in a three-variable system: Fed total assets, overnight reverse repo (ON RRP) balances, and total stablecoin supply.

The relationship should not be summarized as a simple, always-inverse story. Co-movement flips by regime: strongly positive during COVID-era quantitative easing, near zero during bivariate tightening, and strongly negative during easing (when rates are falling). The statistical evidence for the long-run relationship is strongest during quantitative tightening (trace statistic = 48.18), while the inverse level correlation is strongest during easing (r = −0.98).

The strongest mechanism-consistent evidence comes from the yield spread between 3-month Treasury bills and on-chain lending rates: past values of that spread help forecast stablecoin supply growth (Granger F = 11.30, p = 0.001), with similar results using either Compound or Aave as the on-chain rate source. (Granger causality is a statistical test for whether past values of one variable help predict another; despite the name, it is not a test of true causation.) Fed and ON RRP shocks jointly account for 13.9% of stablecoin supply variance at 26 weeks (forecast error variance decomposition, or FEVD, a measure of how much of a variable’s forecast variance is explained by shocks to other variables in a vector autoregression), and deviations from the long-run equilibrium have a smoothed half-life of about five weeks.

No Federal Reserve policy announcement effect survives correction for multiple testing (Bonferroni correction across 19 FOMC meetings), so the evidence sits in the slower equilibrium and yield-spread channels, not in high-frequency reactions to rate decisions.

Issuers hold reserves in Treasury bills and keep the spread between what those reserves earn and what they pay holders (nothing, under current U.S. stablecoin law). During tightening, that spread widens and every dollar of stablecoin issued becomes more profitable. During easing, the issuer's margin shrinks, but rate cuts compress the gap between Treasury bill yields and on-chain lending rates, making stablecoin-denominated lending more competitive. The paper treats both channels as consistent with money-market transmission; direct capital-flow measurement is outside the scope here.

This relationship distributes unevenly across gateway types. Institutional gateways (Tier 1 and Tier 2) share the aggregate inverse pattern. Permissionless protocol volumes (Tier 3) trend in the opposite direction, rising alongside decentralized finance usage growth, not in lockstep with the Fed policy series used here. The aggregate number masks a structural split.

What this means: the paper supports a dollar-specific, regime-dependent transmission channel consistent with money-market mechanics. It does not support a blanket claim that stablecoin supply always moves mechanically opposite the Fed's balance sheet.

2. Concentration sits in the routing layer

The CLII classifies regulatory intensity across 19 gateway entities. On Ethereum, the monitored routing surface breaks down as: Tier 2 at 55% of gross volume, Tier 1 at 41%, and Tier 3 at 4%. After netting out internal rebalancing transfers (where gateways move funds between their own wallets, inflating gross volume), Tier 2 falls to 50% and Tier 1 rises to 47%, so the market is closer to regulated/offshore near-parity than the gross numbers suggest.

Tier 1 is effectively a two-firm market: Coinbase accounts for 56% of Tier 1 volume and Circle 44%. Concentration at the tier level (HHI = 5,021, where the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index measures market concentration and values above 2,500 indicate a highly concentrated market by U.S. Department of Justice standards) and entity level (HHI = 2,742 gross, 2,583 net) both exceed the threshold for "highly concentrated."

That concentration extends below the gateway layer. In the monthly counterparty network, unique counterparties fell 25% and cross-gateway transaction-pairs dropped from 5.8% to 2.6% even as volume doubled. One market-making firm (Wintermute) saw its share of identified counterparty volume rise from 1.4% to 19.9%, making intermediary concentration part of the control-layer story alongside gateway concentration.

The same token also routes through materially different control environments by chain. Tier 1 presence is 41% on Ethereum, approximately 11% on Solana, and zero on Tron, where Tier 3 protocols account for 58% of monitored gateway volume. Even USDT routes 10.8% of its monitored Ethereum gateway volume through Tier 1.

What this means: token-level supply tells you who issued the dollar. Gateway-level flow data tell you who routed it, where risk is concentrating, and which control environment applies.

3. Stress outcomes are gateway-contingent

Two stress episodes bracket the range of gateway-level reallocation.

SVB (March 2023). Capital rotated across dollar gateways, without a broad exit from dollar-pegged instruments. Tier 1 share of monitored gateway volume moved from 63% to 13% and then back to 57% after banking-sector interventions. USDC lost $5.1B in five days while USDT gained roughly $3B.

In relative-intensity terms (multiples of each gateway's normal-period volume), Curve 3pool retained 12.35x baseline volume, Coinbase 2.63x, and Circle 1.39x. The sharpest contrast is within Tier 1: Circle and Coinbase had similar CLII scores but very different stress retention, consistent with entity-specific banking exposure mattering more than regulatory tier. Three gateways with zero SVB exposure (MakerDAO, Paxos, Gemini) saw their issued stablecoins depeg through reserve-chain contagion.

The paper is careful about how far to push the SVB result. Those retention ratios are not pure capital-substitution measures (every major gateway saw more gross activity during the stress window), and SVB alone cannot cleanly separate whether users fled Circle's banking exposure or USDC's 12% peg deviation. The event-time placebo analysis (running the same test on non-stress periods) still shows the episode was statistically extreme: the Tier 1 swing was 51.8 percentage points, with a nadir 2.7 standard deviations below normal.

BUSD wind-down (February 2023 onward). This is the cleaner routing test because there was no peg pressure. The New York Department of Financial Services ordered Paxos to stop minting BUSD. Paxos guaranteed 1:1 redemption throughout. Despite that guarantee, $16.1 billion in stablecoin value migrated mainly toward USDT and Binance-adjacent routing, choosing uninterrupted trading access over stronger regulatory protections. Wintermute’s role expanded materially during the migration; the main wallet (0xDBF5E9c5206d0dB70a90108bf936DA60221dC080, Blockscout-verified) concentrated a growing share of post-BUSD routing volume.

Taken together, SVB and BUSD suggest stress routing is driven more by entity-level exposure and routing continuity than by token labels or regulatory tier alone.

What this means: the CLII is useful for mapping where regulatory obligations attach, but compliance posture and stress fragility are separate questions. Assessing both requires measuring at the gateway level.

Full technical abstract

Dollar stablecoins extend the dollar's exchange, store-of-value, and funding functions into blockchain markets, largely outside the Federal Reserve's standard supervisory perimeter. This paper argues the policy-relevant unit is the gateway (the issuer, exchange, or protocol that routes stablecoin value), because the same dollar token inherits different compliance, monetary, and stress properties depending on which gateway processes it. These are descriptive findings; none should be read as causal estimates.

Data. Daily Ethereum gateway transfers (19 entities, 51 addresses, 27.7% of USDC/USDT volume), monthly counterparty networks (15 gateways, 35 months, 3,804 unique transaction-pair relationships), multi-chain flow decomposition across 9 blockchains ($60.6T Artemis-filtered transfer volume), and stress-episode windows around the SVB crisis and the BUSD regulatory action.

Monetary transmission. Stablecoin supply is cointegrated with Fed balance-sheet variables in a regime-dependent pattern consistent with money-market transmission. Cointegration is strongest during quantitative tightening (trace = 48.18) and weaker during easing (trace = 17.71), while inverse level correlations are strongest during easing (r = −0.98). A yield-spread channel provides mechanism-consistent evidence: it forecasts stablecoin supply growth at lag 1 (Granger F = 11.30, p = 0.001). Fed and ON RRP innovations jointly account for 13.9% of stablecoin supply variance at 26 weeks; deviations from the long-run equilibrium have a smoothed half-life of approximately five weeks. No FOMC announcement effect survives Bonferroni correction. The co-movement distributes unevenly: institutional gateways share the aggregate negative correlation (Tier 1 r = −0.56, Tier 2 r = −0.45), while permissionless protocol volumes trend positively (Tier 3 r = +0.37).

Gateway classification. The Control Layer Intensity Index (CLII) classifies regulatory intensity across five weighted dimensions: Regulatory License 25%, Reserve Transparency 20%, Freeze/Blacklist Capability 20%, Compliance Infrastructure 20%, Geographic Restrictions/Sanctions 15%. Tier 1 above 0.75; Tier 2 from 0.30 to 0.75; Tier 3 below 0.30. The CLII measures operational control intensity across routing, custody, and compliance functions. Circle (0.92) and Coinbase (0.85) produced opposite flow retention during SVB (1.39x vs. 2.63x) based on entity-specific banking exposure. Concentration increased as volume grew: unique counterparties declined 25%, cross-gateway connections fell from 5.8% to 2.6%, and Wintermute's share of identified counterparty volume rose from 1.4% to 19.9%.

Stress transmission. Two stress episodes bracket the range of gateway-level reallocation. During SVB, capital rotated across dollar gateways; Tier 1 share collapsed from 63% to 13% and recovered to 57% after banking-sector interventions. Three gateways with zero SVB exposure (MakerDAO, Paxos, Gemini) saw their instruments depeg through reserve-chain contagion. During the BUSD wind-down (no peg pressure), displaced capital migrated to USDT and Binance-adjacent routing, with limited rotation into the highest-CLII venues in the same window. Wintermute’s role expanded materially during the migration; precise volume figures require a dedicated methodology not reconstructed in this paper. In both episodes, the salient variation was entity-level, and capital moved in directions that token-level frameworks would not have predicted.

Deposit displacement. The paper does not find evidence that stablecoin growth displaces bank deposits at current scale (first-differenced correlation = +0.12, near zero). The more relevant finding is that deposit recycling varies sharply by issuer: Tether 3.69% bank deposits, Circle 14.24%, Gemini 100%. The monetary impact of stablecoin growth depends on which issuer absorbs it and which banks hold those reserves.
