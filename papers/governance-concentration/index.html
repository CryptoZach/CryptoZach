Governance Concentration Beyond Token Allocation: An Institutional Design Analysis of DePIN and DeFi

9 min read·April 2026·Zach Zukowski

~30 min read · April 2026 · SSRN 6599278 · DOI 10.2139/ssrn.6599278

Description current as of April 18, 2026. Submitted to Frontiers in Blockchain (Blockchain Economics specialty); editorial assignment pending.

Initial token allocation does not predict where governance concentration ends up. A cross-sectional analysis of 40 protocols (15 DePIN, 15 DeFi, 8 L1/L2 infrastructure, 2 social or stagnated) finds the lead correlation null: Pearson r = 0.19, p = 0.25, N = 37 between initial allocation design and observed governance concentration. The institutional-design story is what does the predictive work. Sector membership matters (Mann-Whitney p = 0.031, Cohen's d = 0.96 between DePIN and DeFi), insider retention amplifies it (Spearman rho = 0.54), subsidy correlations turn out to be entirely Livepeer-driven, and delegation amplifies token concentration into voting concentration by 3 to 6 times in DePIN. The paper applies a five-lens framework (Kantian publicity, Rawlsian legitimacy, Pettit non-domination, Ostromian polycentricity, Hayekian knowledge-use) and a Blockscout-verified exclusion of 69 protocol-controlled addresses across 20 protocols.

Five-lens institutional-design framework

Lens What it asks Operationalized as
Kantian publicity Are the rules public, predictable, and applied uniformly? Disclosure quality; equal access to governance information
Rawlsian legitimacy Do the rules protect the least advantaged participants? Quorum design; minority-protection mechanisms; fair allocation rules
Pettit non-domination Can any single actor dominate decisions or impose arbitrary will? Concentration measurements (HHI, Gini); single-actor veto capacity
Ostromian polycentricity Are governance responsibilities distributed across multiple centers? Multi-body design; delegation patterns; jurisdictional boundaries
Hayekian knowledge-use Does the system use local and dispersed knowledge effectively? Information aggregation; local-decision rights; market signals

Each protocol scored 0 to 2 per dimension (Table 3 in the manuscript). Ostromian polycentricity is the dominant lens by frequency in the v5 manuscript (22 in-text citations).

What this paper changes

Most empirical work on token-protocol governance focuses on initial token allocation as the predictive variable. The intuition: how tokens are distributed at launch determines how decisions are made later. This paper challenges that intuition with a 40-protocol cross-sectional dataset and finds the bivariate correlation between allocation design and observed governance concentration is null. The lead-finding framing is therefore "allocation design null plus four supporting findings", not the earlier "universal covariate null" framing that overgeneralized the result.

The paper also operationalizes a five-lens institutional-design framework (Kantian publicity, Rawlsian legitimacy, Pettit non-domination, Ostromian polycentricity, Hayekian knowledge-use). Each lens generates testable propositions about which design features should reduce concentration. The combined analysis is structural: the paper measures whether the design features each lens recommends actually correlate with the concentration outcomes the lens predicts.

Core findings

1. Allocation design does not predict governance concentration

The lead correlation, Pearson r = 0.19, p = 0.25, N = 37 (Spearman rho = 0.07, p = 0.69) is statistically null. Three of the 40 protocols lack initial allocation data and drop from this test, leaving N = 37. The result holds across alternative measures of allocation concentration and across initial-supply windows.

2. Sector membership predicts concentration

The strongest single covariate. On the 15-DePIN vs 15-DeFi subsample, the Mann-Whitney test of governance-concentration distributions yields p = 0.031 with Cohen's d = 0.96 (a large effect). The sector effect survives a multivariate regression: Table 6 Models 1 and 2 show the DePIN dummy at p = 0.012 with controls for protocol age, market capitalization, and infrastructure subcategory.

Robustness check: 23 of 30 leave-one-out iterations remain significant. The sector effect is not a single-protocol artifact.

3. Insider retention is the operating mechanism

Spearman rho = 0.54 between insider-retention proxies (insiders who continued to hold and exercise governance over time, measured against a snapshot baseline) and observed governance concentration. This is the mechanism that explains why allocation design alone does not predict the outcome: the protocols where allocation translates into concentration are the ones where insiders systematically retain the holdings that produce the votes.

4. The subsidy correlation is a single-outlier observation

The bivariate levels correlation between subsidy intensity and governance concentration is Pearson r = 0.58, p = 0.008, N = 20. That looks strong, but the LOO and rank-based diagnostics deflate it sharply: excluding Livepeer (which sits at 88.5x subsidy intensity, far above the next-highest protocol), r falls to 0.12, p = 0.63; Spearman rho is 0.16, p = 0.51; LOO 19 of 20 iterations significant, all driven by Livepeer's removal toggling the result.

Read this as: there is one anomalous high-subsidy protocol whose presence drives the entire population correlation. The paper treats it as a flagged single-observation result and discourages overinterpreting it as a general subsidy-concentration relationship.

5. Delegation amplification 3 to 6 times in DePIN; heterogeneous in infrastructure

Where token holdings are merely concentrated, delegated voting power is more concentrated, often by 3 to 6 times in DePIN protocols. The mechanism is straightforward: a small number of delegates aggregate voting authority from many token holders, and the concentration of delegated votes therefore exceeds the concentration of underlying tokens.

Infrastructure protocols are heterogeneous. Optimism amplifies at 0.79x (delegation actually disperses voting power, an inverted result), Arbitrum at 4.3x (delegation amplifies). The amplification factor is itself a design choice worth measuring as a separate dimension of governance architecture.

6. Concentration ranges are design-sensitive, not sector-determined

HHI ranges:

  • DeFi: 0.003 (Balancer, BAL) to 0.174 (Curve raw, CRV)
  • DePIN: 0.013 (Morpheus AI) to 0.199 (Livepeer)

Within each sector there is roughly an order of magnitude variation. The sector effect (DePIN higher on average) sits on top of substantial within-sector heterogeneity. The paper's framing is design-sensitive, with the sector as signal and within-sector variation as the operative space for design improvement, not a fully sector-determined story.

7. Lead-finding framing (locked)

The paper's headline result is the allocation null. The four supporting findings (sector significant in bivariate and multivariate tests; insider retention as the mechanism; subsidy LPT-driven; delegation amplification 3 to 6 times in DePIN) frame the why. Avoid the earlier "no covariate predicts governance concentration" or "universal covariate null" framings; they overgeneralized the result and have been retired.

8. On-chain data preferred for DePIN protocols

For DePIN protocols, the analysis uses direct on-chain transaction data (token burns, protocol fee flows) rather than aggregator-derived metrics. This choice favors authoritative source data for the DePIN subset: standardized cross-protocol aggregators do not always capture protocol-native fee structures (burn-and-mint equilibria, bonding-curve mechanics) that contribute meaningfully to DePIN protocol revenue. The paper uses on-chain values where they diverge from aggregator coverage.

Methods and data

Sample

40 protocols total: 15 DePIN, 15 DeFi, 8 L1/L2 infrastructure, and 2 protocols included as social or stagnated cases. Sector membership is the primary stratification variable. Multivariate regression specifications use N = 38 (two protocols are dropped because they are missing covariates required by the specification).

Concentration measures

Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI): a concentration measure summing squared participant shares; higher values indicate more concentrated control. Used as the primary outcome.

Gini coefficient: a measure of distributional inequality ranging from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (maximal inequality). Used as a secondary outcome.

Delegation amplification: the phenomenon where concentration in delegated voting power exceeds concentration in underlying token holdings, because delegates aggregate voting weight from multiple token holders. Computed as the ratio of HHI on delegated voting power to HHI on raw token holdings.

Exclusion methodology

Protocol-controlled addresses are excluded from concentration calculations. These addresses (staking contracts, bridges, custodians, vesting locks, team multisigs, treasuries) hold tokens without exercising governance. Including them inflates measured concentration without reflecting actual decision-making power.

The paper identifies 69 protocol-controlled addresses across 20 protocols, classified through Blockscout verification, holder-pattern analysis, and known-entity matching. Exclusion corrections change affected protocols' HHI by up to 5x. The exclusion methodology is also inherited by the companion paper Who Burns the Tokens? for its GeoDePIN deep-dive.

Five-lens institutional-design framework

The five lenses (enumerated in the framework artifact above) are operationalized through a 0-to-2 scoring rubric (Table 3 in the manuscript). Ostromian polycentricity is the dominant lens by in-text frequency in v5 (22 citations) and is also the framing inherited by the companion paper Who Burns the Tokens?.

Position in the literature

The paper engages four leading research groups on DAO governance concentration: Han, Lee, and Li (Seoul National University and University of Florida); Lin William Cong (Cornell and NTU); Robin Fritsch and collaborators (ETH Zurich); and Appel and Grennan (Boston College and Emory). Section 4.5 (Position in Literature) develops the contrast between a Voshmgir-style design-as-systems framing and the institutional-design thesis the paper advances. The contrast is load-bearing for the paper's argumentative structure.

Limitations

  • Cross-sectional design captures concentration at a snapshot; it does not directly observe how concentration evolves through governance episodes. Time-series extension is identified as future work.
  • The five-lens framework operationalization relies on a 0-to-2 scoring rubric (Table 3). Coding judgments are documented in the replication package, but other researchers using the same lenses might score individual protocols differently.
  • The subsidy correlation result is single-outlier-driven; readers should treat it as a Livepeer-specific observation, not a general population pattern. The paper flags this explicitly.
  • External aggregator benchmarks for DePIN protocols may not always align with the paper's on-chain measurements where protocol-native fee structures are involved; the paper preserves on-chain values as the primary read for the DePIN subset.
  • Insider-retention proxies are constructed from holder-pattern analysis; a richer dataset (off-chain governance participation, signed proposals) would sharpen the mechanism claim in finding 3.
