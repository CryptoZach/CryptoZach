Auditing Governance Concentration Beyond Token Allocation: A Live-Governance Study of 52 Token Protocols

~30 min read · SSRN 6599278 · DOI 10.2139/ssrn.6599278

Description current as of June 18, 2026. Accepted for publication at Frontiers in Blockchain (Blockchain Economics specialty); in production (typesetting and copyediting).

How a token launches does not predict who controls its governance later. Across 52 protocols, what concentrates governance power is post-launch structure (who keeps insider holdings, the protocol's architectural sector, and how delegation is designed), not the initial allocation table.

Token allocation is a launch document. Governance concentration is a live institutional state, and it has to be audited after protocol-controlled addresses are removed, not read off the cap table.

At a glance

Question
Does a token's initial allocation table, who received what at launch, predict who controls its governance today?

Answer
No. Initial allocation is uninformative (insider allocation r = 0.09, p = 0.55, N = 50). What concentrates governance power is post-launch structure: insider retention (Spearman rho = 0.44), architectural sector (DePIN more concentrated than DeFi, Cohen's d = 0.65), and delegation design (13 of 18 protocols amplify it). These are descriptive associations, not causal estimates.

Evidence base
A cross-section of 52 protocols (DePIN, DeFi, L1/L2 infrastructure, social tokens), top-1,000 holders, snapshots collected March to May 2026, after removing 133 protocol-controlled addresses across 38 protocols that hold tokens but structurally cannot vote (a five-class exclusion typology).

Five findings

1

PCA correction is the load-bearing instrument

Naive holder lists include addresses that cannot vote as independent governance actors: staking contracts, exchange custodians, vesting locks, bridges, and treasuries. A five-class protocol-controlled-address (PCA) exclusion removes 133 such addresses across 38 protocols and corrects a median 2.3x (maximum roughly 18x, the RENDER case) inflation.

What this means: Uncorrected, prior holder-list studies measure protocol architecture, not governance control. Every downstream finding depends on cleaning the list first.

2

Launch allocation is uninformative about concentration

Insider allocation is not associated with post-distribution concentration: Pearson r = 0.09, p = 0.55, N = 50. The same holds for every launch-design and financial covariate (team r = -0.03, investor r = 0.19, maturity r = 0.10, float r = 0.03, valuation r = 0.21). This is a bounded null, not a mere failure to find an effect (TOST equivalence p = 0.02 within the powered range).

What this means: Initial tokenomics is metadata about a launch, not evidence about who holds governance power today.

3

Insider-wallet retention is the operating mechanism

Protocols with more insider wallets among their post-exclusion top holders are more concentrated: Spearman rho = 0.44 (p = 0.005, N = 39), robust across all 39 leave-one-out iterations and surviving a non-insider-HHI tautology check (rho = 0.54).

What this means: What concentrates voting power over time is which insiders keep and exercise their holdings, not who received tokens at launch. This is the mechanism behind the allocation null.

4

Sector membership matters, after correction

DePIN governance is more concentrated than DeFi (mean HHI 0.067 vs 0.026, where a higher HHI means more concentrated holdings): Mann-Whitney p = 0.028, Cohen's d = 0.65 on the balanced contrast (uniform-exclusion robustness d = 0.75 at p = 0.018; powered model N = 50, log-HHI p = 0.0107). The significance is load-bearing on the full five-class exclusion: retaining exchange-custody wallets attenuates the contrast to the margin of conventional significance.

What this means: Architecture, not launch design, is the structural correlate of where governance concentration lands. It is design-tractable, not destiny: Anyone Protocol and Hivemapper are low-concentration DePIN counterexamples.

5

Delegation usually amplifies concentration, by design

Delegated voting power is more concentrated than underlying holdings in 13 of 18 protocols (2.5x to 25.6x, median 4.4x), because delegates aggregate weight from many holders. Five protocols disperse instead (ENS, GMX, HNT, JUP, LPT), and vote-escrow systems that weight votes by lock duration (Curve, Balancer, Frax) amplify far more, roughly 11x to 21x.

What this means: A delegate program is itself a governance instrument that concentrates or disperses power by design, so it needs its own concentration audit rather than a presumption that delegation deconcentrates.

Request methodology memo

The five-class PCA exclusion typology

Before any covariate can be tested, the holder list has to be cleaned of addresses that hold tokens but structurally cannot vote. A protocol-controlled address (PCA) is one of five classes. The methodology is sample-independent: the specific addresses depend on the protocol, but the typology applies uniformly to any ERC-20 or SPL governance dataset.

Class What it captures Why it cannot vote
1. Burn destinations Canonical and protocol-specific burn addresses (0x0, 0xdead) Tokens are inert by construction (e.g. Uniswap 0xdead, 102.5M UNI)
2. Foundation and treasury custody Foundation multisigs, protocol treasuries, vesting locks Institutional, not community, holdings (the highest-yield class: 61% of exclusions)
3. Staking-aggregation contracts Contracts that pool staker holdings (stkAAVE, sGMX) Not an independent voter; stakers retain pass-through voting power
4. Bridge and migration custody Cross-chain bridges (Wormhole, LayerZero), migration contracts Tokens held in transit or as cross-chain accounting positions
5. Centralized-exchange custody CEX hot and cold wallets holding customer deposits Custodied for many customers; exchanges abstain from governance

133 PCA exclusions across 38 protocols (125 unique addresses; some recur, such as a Binance hot wallet across four tokens). Class 3 versus Class 5 is disambiguated by transaction-pattern signatures where entity labels are unavailable. Median HHI inflation 2.3x; maximum roughly 18x (the RENDER case).

What this paper changes

Most empirical work on token-protocol governance reasons from two objects: the launch allocation table and the raw holder list. Both are the wrong object for live governance power. Token allocation is a launch document; governance concentration is a live institutional state. The contrast tracks the distinction comparative political economy draws between de jure power (authority on paper) and de facto power (authority in practice): the formally specified allocation of authority differs from the power groups actually exercise once a system is live.

This paper builds a 52-protocol cross-section and corrects it first. The measurement instrument that makes every downstream result credible is the protocol-controlled-address (PCA) exclusion: before any covariate can be tested, the holder list is cleaned of addresses that hold tokens but structurally cannot vote. On the corrected data, launch allocation turns out to be uninformative about current concentration, while post-distribution structure (insider retention, architectural sector, and delegate-program design) does the work. The practical implication is a changed audit default. A serious decentralization audit should start from PCA-corrected current holder concentration, then add insider-retention, voting-HHI, and delegation or vote-escrow adjustment. It should not reason from launch allocation tables.

Core findings

1. PCA correction changes what prior holder-list studies measure

Naive token holder lists include addresses that cannot vote as independent governance actors: staking contracts, exchange custodians, vesting locks, bridges, and protocol treasuries. Across 38 protocols, 133 such protocol-controlled-address exclusions (125 unique addresses; some recur, such as a Binance hot wallet across four tokens) inflated affected protocols' HHI in prior measurements. The overstatement reached up to roughly 18x for RENDER, where Wormhole bridge custody dominated the naive top-1,000, with a median inflation factor of 2.3x across the 32 protocols that have complete pre-exclusion and post-exclusion data. The correction is not a refinement of prior estimates; it changes what they measure, from protocol architecture to governance control.

2. Launch allocation is uninformative about concentration

Initial insider allocation is not associated with post-distribution concentration: Pearson r = 0.09, p = 0.55, N = 50 (Spearman rho = 0.19, p = 0.18). The result is not specific to one measure. Team allocation (r = -0.03), investor allocation (r = 0.19), protocol maturity (r = 0.10), circulating-to-total supply (r = 0.03), and market-cap-to-fully-diluted-valuation (FDV) (r = 0.21) are each uninformative as well. The allocation battery runs on the N = 50 sectors-only sample (the full insider-populated set is N = 51). This is a bounded null, not a mere failure to find an effect: a two-one-sided-tests (TOST) equivalence procedure shows the correlation is statistically equivalent to zero within the range the sample is powered to detect (TOST p = 0.02 within |r| = 0.38). A joint launch-design block (team, investor, maturity) explains 4.2 percent of HHI variance (F(3, 41) = 0.60, p = 0.62). An initial 24-month temporal endpoint correlation is also descriptively consistent with the null (r = -0.14, N = 13). Initial tokenomics is metadata about a launch, not evidence about who holds governance power today.

3. Insider-wallet retention is the operating mechanism

Protocols with more insider wallets among their post-exclusion top holders are more concentrated: Spearman rho = 0.44, p = 0.005, N = 39, significant in all 39 leave-one-out iterations. A non-insider HHI tautology check confirms the relationship is more than mechanical (count-based rho = 0.54, p = 0.001, N = 34), and a within-sector partial rank correlation (r = 0.47, p = 0.005, N = 34) shows the retention signal is not absorbed by the sector dummy. This is the mechanism that explains the allocation null: what concentrates voting power over time is which insiders keep and exercise their holdings, not who received tokens at launch.

4. Sector membership matters, conditional on the exchange-custody exclusion

DePIN governance is more concentrated than DeFi after correction (mean HHI 0.067 vs 0.026): Mann-Whitney p = 0.028, Cohen's d = 0.65 on the balanced 15-vs-15 contrast under the voter-inclusive staking pass-through treatment of record; the uniform staking-aggregation exclusion robustness check gives d = 0.75 at p = 0.018, significant on every test. The direction is robust across all 30 leave-one-out iterations, a 100,000-reassignment permutation test (p = 0.009 under the uniform exclusion), bootstrap intervals that exclude zero, the directionally consistent full-frame contrast (15 DePIN vs 24 DeFi; significance there is sensitive to the staking-aggregation treatment), and a powered multivariate model (N = 50, log-HHI p = 0.0107).

The paper is candid about one dependency: the direction of the result holds regardless, but its statistical significance rests on the full five-class exclusion typology. Retaining centralized-exchange custody wallets (dropping Class 5) attenuates the balanced contrast to a medium effect (Cohen's d of approximately 0.62) at the margin of conventional significance (p of approximately 0.05 under the most complete exchange identification). Exchange-deposit concentration is sector-similar, so it masks the underlying governance-holder difference until it is removed. The result is reported as load-bearing on this measurement choice rather than as a settled refinement.

5. The subsidy correlation is a single Livepeer outlier

One financial covariate does show a raw association: subsidy intensity, the rate at which a protocol funds operations from token issuance rather than revenue. The levels correlation between subsidy intensity and concentration is Pearson r = 0.62, p = 0.002, N = 23. It vanishes once Livepeer is excluded. Livepeer sits at 88.5x subsidy intensity, a 3.5-sigma outlier far above any other protocol: without it, r falls to 0.07, p = 0.76, N = 22. A Token Terminal robustness sample returns r = 0.12, p = 0.61, and relaxing the non-zero filter to include twelve genuine-zero-subsidy protocols (N = 35) reproduces the same pattern (r = 0.61 with Livepeer, r = 0.09 without). The paper treats subsidy as a non-generalizing single-observation association, not a population pattern. Financial sustainability is neither necessary nor sufficient for governance decentralization.

6. Delegation usually amplifies concentration, and the magnitude is a design choice

Where token holdings are concentrated, delegated voting power is usually more concentrated still, because a small number of delegates aggregate voting authority from many holders. Across the 18 protocols with sufficient governance data, delegation amplifies concentration in 13, by 2.5x (Gnosis) to 25.6x (Polkadot), with a median near 4.4x. Infrastructure protocols were framed in earlier work as dispersers, but they amplify once non-voting addresses are excluded consistently (Optimism 3.6x, Arbitrum 3.1x).

Five protocols disperse instead of amplify (ENS, GMX, HNT, JUP, LPT), reflecting mature-delegate-program, vote-weight-mechanism, or bonded-stake-delegation designs that push voting power below the holding baseline. Vote-escrow systems form a separate, more extreme class: Curve, Balancer, and Frax amplify by roughly 15x, 21x, and 11.4x through lock-duration weighting. A delegate program is itself a governance instrument that concentrates or disperses power by design, so it needs a concentration audit of its own rather than a presumption that delegation deconcentrates.

7. Concentration spans two orders of magnitude

Post-exclusion holding HHI ranges from 0.005 (Hyperliquid) to 0.199 (Livepeer), with a median near 0.04. The highest concentrations cluster in DePIN (Livepeer 0.199, WeatherXM 0.148, Aethir 0.100, Helium 0.099); DeFi and L1/L2 protocols sit predominantly below 0.05. No protocol exceeds the 0.25 HHI antitrust benchmark (DOJ Horizontal Merger Guidelines), reported here for descriptive interpretation only: a market-concentration threshold is not a democratic-legitimacy threshold. Low-concentration DePIN counterexamples (Anyone Protocol, Hivemapper) make the sector pattern design-tractable rather than sector destiny.

8. Measuring DePIN revenue on-chain

A measurement consideration surfaced during validation. Aggregator revenue coverage is most fully developed for DeFi, where Token Terminal's standardized pipelines capture essentially the full sector; DePIN is a younger category where some protocols earn revenue directly on-chain in native or ETH-denominated forms that aggregator pipelines are still incorporating. Livepeer is the worked case: its revenue arrives as ETH-denominated TicketBroker transcoding fees, so the paper measures it on-chain at approximately $839K (cross-validated against Messari's trailing-twelve-month estimate of $1.17M to $1.43M). The 88.5x Livepeer subsidy ratio behind finding 5 rests on this on-chain figure, reflecting completeness for an emerging sector rather than a correction of any single data source.

Methods and data

Sample

52 protocols spanning DePIN, DeFi, L1/L2 infrastructure, and social-token categories (holder snapshots collected March to May 2026; top-1,000 holders per protocol; concentration computed from Dune Analytics and the Helius DAS API for Solana SPL tokens). Governance HHI by architecture is measured on the 44 governance-token-measured protocols (15 DePIN, 18 DeFi, 11 L1/L2 infrastructure; 2 social tokens excluded from the contrast). The headline DePIN-vs-DeFi test uses a balanced 15-vs-15 draw; the unbalanced full-frame contrast is 15 DePIN vs 24 DeFi; the powered multivariate model uses 50 covariate-complete protocols; voting-HHI is available for 18 protocols with delegate-program data.

Concentration measures

Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI): a concentration measure summing squared participant shares; higher values indicate more concentrated control. The primary outcome, computed on the post-exclusion holder distribution.

Gini coefficient: a measure of distributional inequality from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (maximal inequality). A secondary outcome. HHI and Gini capture distinct properties of the same holder set (Pearson r = 0.52, N = 48), so inequality metrics cannot substitute for direct concentration measurement.

Delegation amplification: the ratio of HHI on delegated voting power to HHI on post-exclusion token holdings. Ratios above 1.0 mean delegation concentrates voting power above the holding baseline; ratios below 1.0 mean it disperses.

PCA exclusion methodology

Protocol-controlled addresses are excluded before any concentration metric is computed. These addresses (burn destinations, foundation and treasury custody, staking-aggregation contracts, bridge and migration custody, and centralized-exchange custody) hold tokens without exercising independent governance, and including them inflates measured concentration.

The paper applies 133 PCA exclusions across 38 protocols via the generalizable five-class typology, identified through Blockscout and Etherscan verification, Nansen entity labels, and transaction-pattern signatures. A separate exchange-custody completion audit removes a further 64 Nansen-labeled centralized-exchange deposit wallets across 21 protocols. Exclusion corrects a median 2.3x (maximum roughly 18x) inflation in affected protocols. The methodology is sample-independent and is also inherited by the companion paper Who Burns the Tokens? for its GeoDePIN deep-dive.

The exclusion discipline extends to the voting layer. Across 13 Snapshot-sourced protocols, holder-side PCA exclusion detects zero PCA voters, because the off-chain accounts that sign Snapshot votes do not overlap with the on-chain holder addresses the typology screens. The voting layer needs its own screen. Four signer-side actors hold substantial voting weight and are registered in a separate signer-side exclusions log: the Compound Foundation (25.01 percent of COMP voting weight), the Balancer DAO multisig (51.95 percent of BAL), the Gitcoin maintainer (27.93 percent of GTC), and a vested WeatherXM insider position (74.04 percent of WXM).

Normative framing and companion papers

The empirical paper is explicitly descriptive: it supplies concentration measurements, not normative adjudication. It frames why those measurements matter through two traditions: republican non-domination (freedom as the absence of subjection to another's arbitrary power) and the Neo-Brandeisian tradition associated with Khan (2017) and Wu (2018). That tradition treats structural power over shared infrastructure as a harm worth scrutiny, independent of whether any single decision has visibly harmed participants. Concentration measured after correction is therefore the object of scrutiny regardless of how holders have so far chosen to use their weight. Concentration of this kind bears on the legitimacy of decentralized governance, not only its efficiency: where a small set of holders or delegates commands decisive voting weight, the broad participation in rule modification that the commons self-governance ideal presumes is nominal rather than operative.

Two companion papers (in preparation) carry the theory the empirical work supports. A theoretical companion develops a Polanyian interpretation: in Karl Polanyi's account, treating land, labor, and money as ordinary tradable goods is a fiction with social costs, and governance rights packaged as transferable tokens function as a candidate fourth such fictitious commodity, and concentration is consistent with the commodification of constitutive political authority rather than a transient failure to finish decentralizing. A normative companion develops the standard by which a given distribution counts as legitimate self-governance, operationalized through Ostrom's design principles for enduring commons and constrained by allocative justice.

Position in the literature

Prior holder-list concentration studies (Barbereau et al. 2023; Feichtinger et al. 2023; Cong et al. 2025) document token inequality, voter concentration, participation gaps, and the decentralization illusion, but they measure a contaminated object: raw holder lists inflate HHI by a median factor of 2.3x and up to roughly 18x once protocol-controlled addresses are removed. The delegation literature (Fritsch, Müller, and Wattenhofer 2024) applies Nakamoto and Gini measures to Compound, Uniswap, and ENS delegate voting power; this paper extends it with delegation-adjusted HHI across 18 protocols. The DePIN scoping literature (Alshater 2026; Nabben et al. 2024) maps the tokenomics design space and identifies the absence of rigorous quantitative empirical work as the field's most pressing gap, which the 52-protocol measurement here responds to directly.

Five forward predictions, pre-registered before extension

The single-snapshot boundary is treated as a design choice rather than a defect: the paper converts the descriptive cross-section into a falsifiable forward agenda. Each prediction carries an explicit falsification threshold, so a future panel can confirm or refute it. (1) Sector persistence: DePIN protocols launching in 2026-2027 will exhibit higher post-distribution holding HHI than same-period DeFi launches. (2) Delegation-amplification universality: any protocol introducing a formal delegate program will produce voting HHI above holding HHI within twelve months. (3) Insider-retention persistence: within-protocol changes in insider fraction will track changes in holding HHI. (4) Delegate-program design as moderator: broad-community-delegate designs will show lower amplification ratios. (5) Chain architecture as trajectory moderator: a maturity-matched Solana-vs-EVM cohort discriminates ecosystem effects from age effects. Hypothesis specifications, statistical tests, and falsification thresholds will be deposited on the Open Science Framework before any panel-data or event-study extension.

Limitations

  • A single 2026 cross-section (holder snapshots collected March to May 2026) supports descriptive association, not causal or trajectory claims. An initial 14-protocol quarterly panel (11 of 14 monotonic HHI decay) is reported as supplementary; a full 52-protocol monthly panel remains future work.
  • The statistical significance, though not the direction, of the sector contrast is load-bearing on the full five-class exclusion typology: retaining exchange-custody wallets attenuates the balanced contrast to the margin of conventional significance (p of approximately 0.05). A reader who prefers to retain those wallets should treat the sector difference as directional rather than conventionally significant.
  • Sector assignment at the DePIN / DeFi / infrastructure-L1 boundary is a researcher judgment call (most visibly Bittensor and Filecoin), and the balanced 15-vs-15 draw is itself a measurement choice; the unbalanced full-frame contrast and the powered N = 50 model reproduce a significant DePIN coefficient without the balanced draw.
  • The hand-classified layers (the five-class PCA typology, the behavioral-signature exchange-custody attribution, and the sector assignment) were coded by a single research team without a formal second-coder inter-rater-reliability statistic, mitigated by external entity-label grounding (Nansen, Etherscan, Blockscout), leave-one-out and permutation checks, and two published sensitivity sweeps.
  • Operator-attribution coverage is asymmetric across chain architectures: roughly 95% on EVM, 80% on Solana, and 53% on Substrate (Polkadot), so cross-protocol attribution findings are reported as lower bounds for Substrate protocols.
  • The sample covers roughly one-third of DeFi and one-fifth to one-quarter of DePIN category market capitalization, skewing toward larger, better-capitalized protocols; voting-HHI is available for only 18 protocols, and the subsidy result rests on a single observation.
